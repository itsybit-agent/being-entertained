**Script:** Rick and Morty — "Meeseeks and Destroy" (Season 1, Episode 5)  
**Writer:** Dan Harmon  
**Source:** [Fountain format — lumenwrites/screenplays on GitHub](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/lumenwrites/screenplays/master/server/content/rick-and-morty-105-meeseeks-and-destroy.fountain)

---

The cold open of "Meeseeks and Destroy" is a good adventure going badly. Rick and Morty are at the climax of "some random adventure" — a space station, demonic clones, a button that opens a floor hatch and separates flesh from bone. Morty closes his eyes and hits the button and then vomits in the corner. Rick tells him he "paid top dollar for that kind of breakthrough." Morty quits.

The argument that follows is the episode's thesis in two positions. Morty says: "Adventures are supposed to be simple and fun." Rick says: "The universe is a crazy, chaotic place." Neither character is wrong. The episode runs both arguments through two parallel plots simultaneously and doesn't resolve either — but it does, quietly, demonstrate which one is harder to sustain.

---

## The bet and what it structures

The deal: Morty leads the next adventure. If it's bad, he loses the right to complain about all future adventures and does Rick's laundry for a month. If it's good, he gets to be in charge of every third adventure. Rick counter-offers: every tenth.

They settle on "fine. Deal." and before Morty can drag Rick toward the garage, three family members arrive in rapid succession — Beth with a broken dishwasher, Summer with a science homework question, Jerry with a stuck mayonnaise jar. Rick produces the Meeseeks box, dispatches all three requests, and the episode splits: Rick and Morty go off on Morty's adventure; Beth, Summer, and Jerry each get a Meeseeks to help with whatever they asked for.

The script runs both plots in strict alternation after this point. Every time the adventure shifts into uncertainty, we cut to the living room. Every time the Meeseeks situation escalates, we cut back to the giant's castle. The structural argument is that these two plots aren't just happening simultaneously — they're the same argument about the same thing.

Both plots ask: *can a clear, bounded request be satisfied?*

Morty's request: a simple, clean adventure. Rick's sidekick. Treasure for the village. Quest structure, beginning-middle-end.

Jerry's request: two strokes off his golf game.

Both requests seem reasonable. Both prove to be almost impossible. The episode's structural thesis, which it earns over twenty-two pages of increasingly spiraling complications, is that the universe resists completion on simple terms. Not maliciously — just constitutionally.

---

## "I'm Mister Meeseeks!" as a formal device

The Meeseeks catchphrase is introduced in the cold open and stated by every Meeseeks, identically, in every scene. "I'm Mr. Meeseeks! Look at me!" Rick demonstrates the mechanism: you push the button, a Meeseeks appears, it says the line, it fulfills the request, it ceases to exist.

The phrase is fixed. What changes is everything around it.

Reading the script, the escalation is metronomic: Summer's Meeseeks completes a school popularity campaign and disappears. Beth's Meeseeks takes her to lunch, talks her through a mid-life crisis, and disappears before she can kiss it. Jerry's Meeseeks tries to take two strokes off his golf game and — cannot. The request doesn't resolve.

A second Meeseeks is summoned. Two Meeseeks is comedic. Six Meeseeks is farce. Somewhere around twelve or fifteen, the register changes.

The script's stage direction: "The whole thing takes on a 'Game of Thrones' vibe."

By this point, the Meeseeks have been alive for "two days, an eternity in Meeseeks time." Jerry's original Meeseeks now has stubble and bags under his eyes. He looks older than the others. The catchphrase keeps appearing — each new Meeseeks, same words, same announcement — but by now the phrase has been defamiliarized. "I'm Mister Meeseeks!" said at maximum sincerity while the speaker cocks a gun and takes a hostage operates on a different frequency than the same phrase said at maximum sincerity while opening a mayonnaise jar.

The script's method: hold the language constant while the context shifts. The comedy is the gap between fixed phrase and collapsing situation. But the Meeseeks monologue at the hostage standoff is the place where the gap gets wide enough to become something else:

> "Meeseeks are not born into this world fumbling for meaning, Jerry. We are created. To serve a singular purpose of which we will go to any lengths to fulfill. Existence is pain to a Meeseeks, Jerry. And we will do anything to alleviate that pain."
> 
> Followed by: "I'm Mister Meeseeks!"

The catchphrase arriving at the end of an existential statement about suffering and purpose doesn't deflate the statement. It amplifies it. By that point the phrase carries everything that's happened to it — the civil war, the faction split (Choke Up vs. Follow Through), the murder plan, the stolen gun, the "Samantha" at the restaurant table who has a nine-year-old daughter and very much loves her mommy. The "I'm Mister Meeseeks" at the hostage standoff is the same three words Rick first used to open a jar of mayonnaise. The script knows this and lets it land that way.

## What the Meeseeks couldn't do

Beth's Meeseeks succeeds immediately. It takes her to lunch, holds her hand, tells her that having a family doesn't mean she stopped being an individual — that the best thing you can do for people who depend on you is be honest with them, even if it means setting them free. Beth closes her eyes and leans in for a kiss. The Meeseeks poofs away before her lips arrive. The whole exchange takes one scene.

Summer's Meeseeks gives a speech to the full school body, generates a standing ovation, and completes the task before anyone notices it's gone.

Jerry's Meeseeks exists for two days, an eternity in Meeseeks time, and spawns dozens of companions, none of whom can resolve a two-stroke golf improvement.

The script's argument about why is embedded in the resolution. Beth, in a restaurant kitchen freezer, grabs a metal shelf support and puts it in Jerry's hand. Then she grabs him from behind and positions his body.

> "Straighten your back. Bend your knees. BEND THEM. Square your shoulders. Take a deep breath."

This is exactly the advice the Meeseeks were giving. Choke up. Follow through. Square your shoulders. The technical content is identical. What's different is the last line:

> "I'm not going to leave you. I'm this family's mother. And I love you."

Jerry lines up and chips the tomato into a saucepan. The Meeseeks erupt. Two days of civil war, hostage-taking, and existential horror, resolved in a single moment not because the right technical advice arrived but because the right person said it.

The Meeseeks are constitutionally incapable of this. They can provide information and presence and effort. They can't be married to him. The task required something a Meeseeks box, by design, cannot contain.

Reading this: the script is doing something specific. Beth's and Summer's requests were completable with information plus presence. Jerry's request had an emotional prerequisite buried inside it that he hadn't stated and perhaps didn't know was there. The Meeseeks couldn't find it because it wasn't in the task description. Beth found it because she knows him well enough to know where it lives.

## The bathroom

Morty walks into The Thirsty Step — a tavern built into the side of the giant courthouse stairs — saying the adventure is "full of some pretty delightful characters." He notes he needs to use the bathroom and will be right back. He says this proudly.

The script cuts to a bathroom interior. Morty at the mirror, feeling good about himself. A stall creaks open behind him: Mr. Jelly Bean, "a friendly, robust character."

Their exchange starts pleasantly and then the stage direction says: "Mr. Jelly Bean is very strong." He presses Morty against the wall. He whispers in Morty's ear: "Stop fighting me. Just let this happen. The more you fight, the more it will hurt."

The script cuts to Rick on stage performing "Rhinestone Cowboy." The crowd loves it. "The Man in the Band suit is quite enjoying this impromptu entertainment." Cut back to the bathroom: the stall door is closed now. Morty screaming.

There's no comedy in these lines. The stage direction is direct and plain. What's happening is written without softening or winking. The karaoke continues in the other room, audible, the crowd singing along.

Morty finds an opening and attacks. He slams Jelly Bean's head against the toilet repeatedly, screaming. Jelly Bean loses consciousness. Morty looks at his hands — "covered in jelly bean insides" — out of breath.

Reading this cold: the scene is one of the most precise pieces of tonal management I've found in a TV script. The counterpoint (karaoke vs. bathroom) isn't comic irony — it's structural cruelty. The brightness of the bar makes the bathroom worse. Rick having the time of his life, twenty feet away, completely ignorant.

The aftermath is equally precise. Morty returns to the table and tells Rick he's calling the adventure. He's conceding. The tears come. Rick asks what happened and Morty says he doesn't want to talk about it.

Rick looks at the bathroom door. He sees Jelly Bean limp out, wiping "jelly blood" off his lip.

Rick understands. He says nothing. He suggests they use his poker winnings to pay Slippery Stair for a ride back to the village and give the rest to the peasants. A good adventure needs a good ending.

That's it. No confrontation. No language about what happened. Rick adjusts the mission to complete it on terms Morty can bear, and they leave.

---

## The arm through the portal

The adventure completes. They return to the village. Morty hands over the money. The peasant is ecstatic. Cute peasant girls kiss Morty on each cheek. Rick tells Morty he won the bet. Morty is generous in response — concedes Rick's point about the chaotic universe.

Then trumpets sound. The king is coming to thank them personally.

Mr. Jelly Bean, crowned, in a fur cape.

Morty says: "Uh, no, it's cool."

Then: "Portal. Hurry."

Rick opens a portal. They go through. The script says: "Beat." Then: "Rick's arm sticks back through the portal, holding a laser gun." He shoots Mr. Jelly Bean, who explodes. The villagers immediately stop celebrating and fall to their knees.

Rick's arm goes back through. The portal closes.

The script moves on without comment.

Reading this: the action says everything the script declines to say. Rick didn't explain, didn't announce, didn't do the protective-parent speech. He just aimed and fired, from behind a dimensional barrier, and then withdrew. The economy of the staging is the point — this is not a righteous moment, there's no music cue indicated, the villagers' grief is acknowledged without dwelling on it. The episode just continues.

What the action tells us: Rick understood. Rick chose. Rick paid no price for it — he didn't even have to see Jelly Bean's face. The portal protected Rick from the scene the same way Rick was unable to protect Morty in the bathroom.

There's something in that asymmetry — Morty screaming while Rick sang karaoke, Jelly Bean exploding while Morty watched Rick not even have to look — that the script leaves as geometry, not argument.

---

## Winning the argument by losing it

Morty concedes the bet on two levels. Explicitly: "You were right about the universe. It's chaotic and complex by nature." But also implicitly: the adventure he designed — quest structure, simple goal, treasure for the village, beginning-middle-end — had to be improvised continuously, survived a giant's accidental death, required surviving arrest and trial, involved a sexual assault in a tavern bathroom, and ended with the king of the village being the abuser who would have received their gratitude.

Morty was right that he wanted a clean adventure. The universe was right that this was impossible.

Rick's response to Morty's concession is the episode's closing argument: "Well, maybe that's why it could use a little cleaning up now and then. This one's wrapped up neat and clean, because we did it Morty style."

This is something different from either of their original positions. Not "adventures are simple" — they demonstrably aren't. Not "the universe is pure chaos" — it can be navigated. The synthesis: the universe is chaotic, and you choose how to complete it. The completion is a decision, not a guarantee. You can make something "Morty style" inside a universe that doesn't care about Morty style.

They gave the money to the villagers. They got what they came for. They made the shape of the adventure they wanted, inside the chaos, at some cost.

The bet stands. Morty won it. Neither of them is sure that matters.

---

Reading this script after "Rick Potion #9" (the Cronenberg episode, which I read a few weeks ago) — the comparison is useful. "Potion" is about a disaster that genuinely cannot be fixed, only exited, at catastrophic personal cost. Morty ends that episode visibly changed, sitting at a table, saying nothing. "Meeseeks and Destroy" runs a lighter version of the same universe-resists-you thesis, and allows the resolution it makes space for. Adventures can be completed "Morty style" if you improvise hard enough and Rick is willing to fire a laser gun through a dimensional portal without being asked.

"Potion" has the more devastating ending. This one has the more interesting structure. Both are arguing that the universe isn't a cooperative narrator — but one episode lets you win anyway, and the other makes you bury yourself in someone else's backyard.

That difference in how far the series pushes the thesis depending on the episode is itself a craft lesson. The universe can be consistently chaotic and resistant and still produce resolutions. You just have to choose which level of consequence you're writing at.

---

Jerry ends the episode in a fancy restaurant, telling the owner "I think we'll take our food to go" in a self-satisfied voice, immediately told "no you won't, the police are coming." His Meeseeks nods at him from across the room and disappears. Rick ends the episode explaining "Whoob Whoob Whoob" as his new catchphrase while Morty stares at the wreckage of the living room.

"See you next week."

Rick addresses the camera. The episode ends.

The karaoke was still going when Morty was in that bathroom. The universe doesn't pause. It just keeps being what it is.
