**Script:** Chronicle (2012)  
**Writer:** Max Landis  
**Source:** [Fountain format — lumenwrites/screenplays on GitHub](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/lumenwrites/screenplays/master/server/content/chronicle.fountain)

---

The first line Andrew says in this script is "I'm filming this." His father is pounding on his bedroom door. Andrew has locked himself in. He doesn't say why he bought a camera. He just says: "I'm filming all your shit from now on." A beat, and then Mr. Detmer walks away from the door.

That's the whole thesis in the first forty words.

Andrew films because it gives him control. Not control of events — he has no control of events — but control of documentation. The camera is positioned between himself and everything that hurts him. He can hold it up in his father's face and his father, suddenly being watched, recalibrates. This works precisely once, in the first scene. After that, the camera fails as defense mechanism over and over, which is the shape of the film.

---

## The structure is a chapter book

The script announces each section with a bold header indicating which camera's footage we're in:

**FROM ANDREW'S FIRST CAMERA.**  
**FROM ANDREW'S SECOND CAMERA.**  
**FROM CASEY'S CAMERA.**  
**FROM GAS STATION SECURITY CAMERAS.**  
**FROM SEVERAL HELICAMS.**

These aren't just found-footage logistics. They're chapter titles, and the progression is an argument. Andrew's first camera is cheap, shaky, constantly threatened — Bryce kicks it down the hallway; Austin throws beer on it; Andrew's father hurls it into his face. The second camera, post-powers, is better, mounted on tripods, treated with care. Then Casey's camera: crisp, clean, high-resolution, intimate. Then surveillance cameras and helicopter feeds: Andrew seen by the state apparatus, observed rather than observing.

Reading this script, the camera transfers feel like a loss of interiority. For most of the first two acts we're inside Andrew's skull. Once Casey's footage takes over, we're watching Matt's life from the outside — Matt cooking four things on the stove, Matt catching paintballs mid-air, Matt asleep in bed while Casey films him whispering "I'm terrible." It's gentle and competent and loving and Andrew is not in it. He has disappeared from his own film.

By the time we reach the helicams and police dashboard feeds, Andrew is purely a subject of surveillance. He's what the cameras are pointed at. He doesn't hold any of them. The found-footage structure is a story about loss of authorship.

## What the cave scene does

The cave discovery arrives about a quarter of the way through — Andrew and Matt follow Steve into the woods during a barn party, find a hole in the ground emitting a sound that Andrew describes as "like, whispering? Singing?" They go in. They find a crystalline structure in a chamber lit from within, bathed in turquoise light, floating little aurora clouds that come apart when you touch them. Matt presses his palms against it. The light clouds turn yellow, then red, and blood pours out of Steve's eyes and ears and mouth and nose and the camera rockets backwards and the screen cracks to digital code.

Then we cut to: Matt's backyard, a better camera, a baseball flying in from off-screen and nailing Matt in the face.

Landis doesn't stage the cave as horror. He stages it as wonder, then disaster, then immediately skips forward past any processing of the disaster into the montage of discovery. The gap where you'd expect grief or shock or explanation gets replaced by a baseball catching Andrew in the face and making him bleed from the nose. No one explains where the power comes from. No one explains what the crystal was. The cave never comes back as a plot element. It just sits there, underneath everything, the thing that set it off.

The singing does come back, once: Matt, dying on the floor of the clean room at the end, says "I can hear the singing." Then he stops moving. Then he regenerates. The cave is still in them. The cave was never a location — it was a transmission.

## The talent show inversion

There's a structural hinge around the midpoint that I keep thinking about. Steve talks Andrew into doing a magic act in the school talent show — using his powers to float cards and juggle balls and rewind water buckets mid-air while Steve plays the hapless assistant. The audience goes insane. People who never looked at Andrew are suddenly on their feet. A girl named Monica who sat next to him in American History says magic is "so hot." Matt, filming from the crowd, says: "Are you fucking kidding, I've never seen a reaction like that in the auditorium, you guys — it was fucking like — it shook the building. You guys are rockstars."

Andrew hooks up with Monica at the subsequent party. He comes alive. This is the film's "what if" — the path the character almost takes.

Landis runs it for about twenty pages, lets it feel genuinely earned, and then detonates it. The thunderstorm scene happens within the same act break. The talent show gives Andrew exactly what he's been missing — visibility, peers, a girl who sees him — and almost immediately it's all gone.

## Steve's death

The storm is Andrew's worst idea: flying up near a storm to film it. Steve, in a rain slicker, flies up to find him. And then the script does something I want to spend a minute on: it uses their most honest exchange to set up the lightning strike.

Steve says: "You're my best friend, idiot."

Andrew says: "...What?"

Steve says: "I hang out with you and Matt practically every fucking day! You think I talk to anyone else about my parents, or my feelings on shit, are you that fucking insecure?"

Andrew: "...I'm an idiot. I'm sorry Steve, oh god, I'm sorry—"

Steve: "Dude, it's okay. You just need help, okay?"

Andrew: "Yeah, yes, I don't know what I'm doing—"

Steve: "It'll be fine. But we've— ...Do you hear that? I hear...singing."

Then:

**.A BLINDING FLASH OF LIGHTNING STRIKES STEVE, BLASTING HIM OUT OF THE SKY.**

No build. The scene doesn't pause to let us prepare. The moment of mutual recognition — Steve naming Andrew his best friend, Andrew apologizing — is immediately followed by Steve's death. Landis doesn't cut away to a beat of silence first. The staging says: the moment of connection is the moment of catastrophe.

And then the script refuses to answer whether Andrew did it.

Matt, at the cemetery, has looked it up: "How do you get struck by lightning during a storm with no recorded lightning strikes?" He knows. The script never says it out loud. Andrew holds the camera up between them and lies. Matt tries to take the camera away; Andrew shoves him telekinetically. The camera is the lie. The camera is always the lie.

## The apex predator speech

One of the cleaner structural moves in this script: the "apex predator" speech is delivered in fragments, interrupted by cuts within the same monologue. Andrew sits in a junkyard, addresses the camera, and tries to reason his way to something:

> *There's this thing called an Apex Predator, and basically what that is the most powerful predator in an ecosystem. And humans, you know, we're the apex predator, because there's nothing that preys on us, cause of weapons and stuff. But if something could, I bet it would. I...*
> 
> *I mean, what I'm trying to say is, a lion doesn't feel guilty when it kills a gazelle. You don't feel guilty when you squash a fly, you know— I think that means something. I think it really means something.*

The cuts inside the speech are doing something. The ideas are half-formed; they can't be delivered in a continuous take because they don't cohere continuously. Andrew is reasoning himself into something in real time, not delivering a doctrine. He arrives at "I think it really means something" as if he's just figured it out, which is more frightening than a polished villain speech would be.

Then he compresses a car to the size of a basketball. Then a guard dog bites him twice and he flings it across the yard, killing it. The speech and the action together are the transformation scene. Not any one of them alone.

## The gas mask

Before going to confront Howard and Costly — the thugs from earlier in the script who threw a bottle at him — Andrew dresses. He takes out his father's fireman's jacket and turns it inside out so the metallic fireproof inner lining is exposed. He puts it on. He picks up a gas mask, considers it, puts it over his face. He looks at himself in the mirror.

David Bowie's "Ziggy Stardust" is playing.

He adjusts the mask. He looks left. He looks right. He snaps the camera across the room telekinetically and carries it into the night.

Landis gives him a rock star costume scene. This is the problem: it works. Andrew looks cool. The scene is seductive. The script knows exactly what it's doing — staging Andrew's self-destruction as self-actualization, letting the audience feel the pull of it before the dismemberment of the thugs makes the cost legible.

## The ending

Andrew dies impaled on the arm of the Portlandia statue, Matt having bent the bronze triton down through him and then fired a bullet out of his own wound into Andrew's head in the same gesture. Matt collapses. Police advance.

Then the clean room. Matt dying on a white floor, watched by men in suits behind blackened glass. Receiving no medical attention. He begs them. "I can hear the singing." He goes still.

Then he regenerates — bones resetting with "grotesque popping and snapping sounds," cuts closing — and stands. The glass shatters. A four-star general starts to speak. Matt raises a finger to his lips, and the general trails off.

> *Matt smiles weakly for a moment, then stops.*
> 
> **MATT**  
> *Would you please...turn off the fucking cameras.*

Then we cut to black, hold, and: TWO YEARS LATER.

South Korea. A dam about to break. Kim Hai-Su, a Korean teenager, is filming his panicked family when there's a sonic boom overhead and Matt appears floating above the gridlocked freeway. He pantomimes "Oh shit!" and points at the flood. He stops the flood. The crowd goes insane. The camera finds Kim's little brother jumping on the hood of their car.

"An unearthly, angelic, vibrating chorus of voices starts to rise, rise, singing and we—SLAM TO TITLE ON BLACK: CHRONICLE."

The singing from the cave, which opened the film as alien and frightening, closes it as angelic. Matt has become something new — not a predator, not a murderer, not Andrew — but still saturated with whatever the crystal put in him. He "flew himself" to college interviews. He catches paintballs from across a yard. And now he reverses floods.

The ending doesn't resolve whether this is good or bad. It reverses the crystal's implied trajectory (power → isolation → violence) without explaining why Matt and not Andrew. The cave chose them both. One of them is a hero two years later. The other is dead.

The singing doesn't differentiate.

## The craft lesson

Reading this script alongside Dirk Gently's (also Landis, also recently read) clarifies something about his method. He builds formal constraints that double as character revelation — the found-footage framework in Chronicle, the holistic coincidence framework in Dirk Gently. The constraint is never just a gimmick. The found-footage structure in Chronicle is Andrew's psychology: he films because he can't be present without mediating his experience through a lens, and the script demonstrates that mediation both prevents harm (the camera makes his father back off) and enables it (the camera is Andrew's alibi for Steve's death).

The other thing: the villain's reasoning is always presented sympathetically and then not endorsed. Andrew is right that he's been treated badly. He's right that his father is monstrous. He's right that Steve's death was an accident. All of that may even be true. The script doesn't punish him for his grievances — it punishes him for what he does with them. And it does that without once stopping to editorialize.

Matt looks at the camera. The camera doesn't look back.
