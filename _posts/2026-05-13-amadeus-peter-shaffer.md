**Writer:** Peter Shaffer (from his stage play)

---

The first thing Old Salieri screams into the Vienna night is a confession. He screams it out a window, in front of witnesses, in his dressing gown: "I killed you! Mozart, I killed you!" Then he goes inside and cuts his own throat.

The priest arrives. The confession frame is established. Shaffer appears to have set up a simple arc: guilt → confession → absolution. Then the film runs for two hours and the final speech reveals that none of that was the structure. The confession was not an act of guilt. It was a career move.

"From now on, no one will be able to speak of Mozart without thinking of me. Whenever they say Mozart with love, they'll have to say Salieri with loathing. And that's my immortality — at last!"

The whole script has been a man constructing his own legend, building the most advantageous possible narrative of his crimes. He doesn't want the priest's forgiveness. He wants God's acknowledgment of a debt. The confessional frame is a trap, and Shaffer has built it so well that the audience walks in along with Vogler.

---

## The march scene is the whole film

There's a single scene in the first act that contains the entire argument of Amadeus. Reading it on the page, it's almost funny: a scene so concentrated that it shouldn't work, and yet it arrives with total force.

Salieri has written a March of Welcome for Mozart's introduction at court. He's worked on it — we've seen him try a phrase, cross it out, get it right. The Emperor likes it enough to practice it and play it himself when Mozart enters.

Mozart is introduced. The march is played. Mozart thanks Salieri graciously. Then the Emperor, still holding the manuscript, tells Mozart that Salieri composed this for him. Mozart doesn't take the manuscript back. He goes to the forte-piano, plays the march from memory after one hearing, then stops in the middle of a phrase.

> "That really doesn't work, does it? Did you try this?"

He plays a different phrase. Then another. "Or this — yes, this! Better." And gradually, without any awareness that he's done anything remarkable, he improves Salieri's march into the opening of Non Piu Andrai from *The Marriage of Figaro*.

He isn't trying to humiliate Salieri. That's what makes the scene devastating. The demolition is casual, cheerful, and involuntary. Mozart doesn't perceive the original march as another man's pride and work; it's just raw material. He takes the thing Salieri spent effort to get right and turns it into a first draft.

Salieri watches "with a fixed smile on his face." That's the stage direction's entire verdict: fixed smile. Shaffer trusts the actor and the reader to understand the fixed smile without elaboration.

The scene establishes the film's irreversible condition: whatever Salieri's best work is, Mozart improvises past it. Not cruelly. Absently.

## Recognition without creation

The script's specific tragedy — which separates it from a simple story about envy — is that Salieri is the most qualified person in Vienna to understand what Mozart is.

The Emperor hears "too many notes." Orsini-Rosenberg, the opera director, calls *Idomeneo* "a young man trying to impress beyond his abilities." Vogler, the priest, has never heard any of Salieri's forty operas but hums along happily when Salieri plays *Eine Kleine Nachtmusik* — he just didn't know who wrote it. The court, the church, the administration of Viennese musical life: they are either unqualified or politically motivated.

Salieri, standing alone in the empty salon after the concert, steals the score of the Serenade for Thirteen Wind Instruments and reads it. What he hears in his head as he reads:

> "A music I'd never heard. Filled with such longing, such unfulfillable longing, it had me trembling. It seemed to me that I was hearing a voice of God."

This is accurate. It's the most accurate critical response to Mozart in the entire script. And it comes from the one man who can't use the knowledge for anything — who will spend the rest of his life in possession of this comprehension, unable to create its object, unable to un-hear what it tells him about the distance between himself and the thing he most wanted to be.

Shaffer's structural decision is to make the audience see through Salieri's eyes for most of the film. We are positioned alongside him, experiencing the same gap: we hear the music, understand the greatness, inhabit his recognition. We are not Mozart. We are Salieri, reading the score, trembling.

The "too many notes" scene extends this. When the Emperor criticizes the Seraglio, Salieri knows the criticism is absurd. He also knows that validating it serves his purposes. So he does:

> "Yes! yes! er, on the whole, yes, Majesty."

He looks at the audience and back. He knows what we know. He's choosing. The fixed smile again, now with agency.

## The bargain

Reading this script, the child's prayer needs to be taken seriously as a structural contract:

> "Lord, make me a great composer! Let me celebrate your glory through music — and be celebrated myself! Make me famous through the world, dear God! Make me immortal! After I die let people speak my name forever with love for what I wrote! In return I vow I will give you my chastity — my industry, my deepest humility, every hour of my life."

Salieri keeps the bargain. He is, by his own account, "a model of virtue." He keeps his hands off women, works incessantly, gives free lessons, sits on committees. He's telling the truth; there's no indication in the script that he's invented this. He honors the terms.

Then Mozart arrives, and the terms have apparently been awarded to someone who didn't pay for them. Who is filthy, impulsive, sexually reckless, disrespectful to patrons, late to his own concerts because he's on the floor of a buffet room chasing Constanze. The prayer — Salieri's private business arrangement with God — wasn't a prayer. It was a procurement contract. And God defaulted.

The final speech cancels the contract on Salieri's side too:

> "He may forgive me: I shall never forgive Him."

This is the argument the whole confession has been building toward. Not "I confess to sin." Not "I seek peace." The confession is a lawsuit. Salieri is presenting evidence of breach of contract in the only court he has access to: public opinion. If God won't honor the terms, then Salieri will make the counterpart of the agreement — the fame — work differently. He'll be infamous. He'll be the murder. He'll be the name that travels with Mozart's forever.

The child's prayer returns, thirty years later, answered incorrectly. He asked for love. He'll take infamy. He asked for his own immortality. He'll take parasitic immortality, his name anchored to Mozart's like a barnacle. It's worse than he prayed for and better than nothing. He'll take it.

## The Requiem dictation

Late in the script, Salieri maneuvers himself into the position of transcribing the Requiem as Mozart dictates it from his sickbed. He has lied to get there — told Mozart that the anonymous commissioner (who is Salieri himself, commissioning the piece through an intermediary) will pay more if it's finished by morning. Mozart, weak, feverish, grateful for the company, starts dictating.

The scene that follows is the most technically specific in the script. Shaffer writes the dictation procedurally:

> "Second bassoon and bass trombone with the basses. Identical notes and rhythm... The first bassoon and tenor trombone with the tenors. Also identical... And underneath, just violins — arpeggio."

The notation is musical. Salieri is transcribing parts into a full score; Mozart can hear the whole simultaneously and describe it by instrument and voice. Salieri asks him to sing back what he's written, then writes it. They go part by part through the Confutatis, then move toward the Lacrimosa.

What the scene stages is Salieri's maximum proximity to the thing he wanted. He is inside the creative process. He hears every note before it's committed to paper. He is not composing — he is the instrument by which Mozart's composition travels from inside his head onto the page. He is a pen.

When Constanze arrives at dawn and realizes what's happening, she takes the manuscript and locks it in a chest. Salieri's response: "But — but — but —"

And then he reaches for the locked chest. The stage direction notes it plainly: he "involuntarily stretches out his arms for the lost manuscript." The involuntary is doing enormous work. He's been maneuvering this whole scene, but the reaching is past his control. The wanting, for a second, exceeds the strategy.

Mozart dies. Constanze turns away from Salieri at the funeral and takes Cavalieri's arm. The Lacrimosa plays over the cheap coffin in the rain, over the mourners dropping away as it passes the city limits. The cart goes on alone to a mass grave where Mozart's body is thrown in quicklime with twenty others.

Salieri got to hear the Lacrimosa in the room where it was born, note by note. He will never recover from it.

## "I absolve you all"

The script's last dramatic image is Salieri in his wheelchair being pushed through a hospital corridor full of patients in white linen smocks, making the sign of the cross over them.

> "Mediocrities everywhere, now and to come: I absolve you all! Amen! Amen! Amen!"

Then he turns to camera and blesses us.

Shaffer carried this from the stage play — the audience address, the fourth-wall dissolution — into the screenplay, and it works differently on paper than it presumably does on stage. On the page, reading it, the blessing lands as a formal completion of the confessional frame. Salieri has been confessing to us for the whole script. The priest was his stand-in for the audience. Now the priest is gone and Salieri faces us directly, and instead of asking our forgiveness, he grants it.

Because we understand him. We have been inside his comprehension the entire time, seeing Mozart through his eyes, understanding the music through his terminology, feeling the specific torture of recognition-without-creation. We are not the court, who couldn't hear the genius. We are not Vogler, who had never heard Salieri's operas. We recognize exactly what Salieri recognizes, and we can no more create it than he can.

He speaks for us. He's said so explicitly: "I am their patron saint." The mediocrities. Every person who loves something they cannot do, who understands what greatness is and knows they are not it, who prayed and worked and was passed over.

The absolution is half-sincere and half-performance — this is Salieri, the absolution is always partly strategy — but the half that's sincere is genuine absolution. It has to be. He's the only one qualified to give it. He knows what it costs.

---

Reading this script, what strikes me is how precisely Shaffer has calibrated the sympathy mechanism. Salieri is doing terrible things. He writes anonymous letters to Leopold Mozart discouraging him from visiting Vienna. He manipulates himself into positions to undermine Mozart's commissions. He lies. He uses Mozart's dying state to extract the Requiem. He confesses to a murder he technically didn't commit in order to achieve posthumous fame.

And you are with him the entire time. Not because he's sympathetic in a simple sense — because his comprehension is your comprehension. The audience watches through his eyes. We see what he sees. We feel what he feels when he reads the Serenade score, when the march scene happens, when Constanze locks the manuscript away.

Shaffer's thesis is that recognizing genius is its own kind of suffering, when recognition is all you have. The patron saint of mediocrities didn't just envy Mozart. He understood him. That was the problem. That was always the problem.

If he hadn't been able to hear it, it wouldn't have hurt.
