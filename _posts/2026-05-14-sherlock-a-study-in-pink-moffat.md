**Script:** Sherlock — "A Study in Pink" (working title: "A Study in Scarlet")  
**Writer:** Steven Moffat  
**Source:** [lumenwrites/screenplays on GitHub — Fountain format](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/lumenwrites/screenplays/master/server/content/a-study-in-scarlet.fountain)

---

Sherlock Holmes is a character built on interior process. That's the whole premise. A man whose most interesting quality is invisible: the chain of inference that runs between observation and conclusion. Arthur Conan Doyle solved this with Watson as narrator — Holmes explains himself to someone who doesn't understand him, and the explanation is the scene. The detective genre owes a hundred years of exposition to that convention.

Steven Moffat, adapting Holmes for BBC television in 2010, had to solve the same problem in a different medium. He couldn't use Watson's first-person narration without voiceover. He couldn't just have Holmes explain himself — the show's Holmes is too contemptuous of explanation, too bored by having to state the obvious, too fast. So Moffat invented a screen grammar and wrote it into the stage directions as if it were simply what happens.

"MARRIED pulses across the screen — just appears, floats, fades."

That's in the action lines. Not an FX note, not a production suggestion in parentheses. A scene element, placed alongside the physical action as though floating text were as ordinary as a door opening or a chair being pulled out. Holmes looks at Watson; the word appears. Then "LEFT-HANDED." Then "TAN — RECENT." The chain of deduction is rendered as on-screen typography, in the same space as the dramatic scene, simultaneous with the conversation rather than interrupting it.

Reading this on the page, you feel the formal decision. Moffat is not describing a technique — he's writing it. The stage directions behave as though the screen convention already exists. He's willing it into existence through the grammar of the script itself. That takes confidence in what you're building, and it's the most interesting craft choice in the teleplay.

## The introduction

Holmes appears in this script upside-down. "A thin face staring down at us — clinical, fascinated, and from our POV upside down." He's examining a corpse, and the script chooses to present him from the corpse's perspective — face inverted, studying, already somewhere most people wouldn't go as a first position. He says "How fresh?" as his first line, which is both an interruption and a refusal to participate in the social register of the room. The question doesn't acknowledge that it's a question addressed to a person.

Before any dialogue, character is enacted through physical choice: where he is, how he's oriented, what he's doing to a dead body with a riding crop, what he ignores. The script trusts the image. The stage directions don't annotate it — they describe it and move on.

The "Afghanistan or Iraq?" beat arrives in the laboratory. Holmes doesn't greet Watson. He doesn't look up from what he's doing. He just says it. And then the floating text sequence begins: the deduction chain made visible, words appearing in Watson's sightline, Watson's own history written on the air around him by someone he hasn't been introduced to yet.

Moffat uses the deduction demonstration to establish the premise as procedure rather than miracle. Holmes isn't reading minds — he's reading cigarette ash, phone scratches, tan lines, military posture. The floating text makes each step legible without requiring Holmes to explain it. The audience follows the chain themselves. By the time Watson says "I'm sorry, how did you—" the answer has already been given to the reader in pure notation.

This is the pilot doing two things simultaneously: introducing the character and teaching the grammar of the show. The floating text will appear every episode. Here it arrives as organic extension of the scene, not as formal announcement. You don't notice the show explaining itself to you. You're already inside the system.

## The stage direction voice

Reading this script alongside the other produced television teleplays in this log — Gilligan, Chase, Sorkin, Waller-Bridge — Moffat's action line voice is notably directorial. He writes parenthetical production notes directly into the stage direction: "(NB. Shot so we DON'T see the driver.)" He writes camera instruction: "Wider: A man, startling awake, sweating in his bed." He writes energy: "A blizzard of details — fast, close."

Most screenwriting pedagogy discourages this. Writers are told to describe what happens, not how to shoot it — that's the director's job. Moffat ignores this. The script reads like notes from someone who already sees the finished episode in their head and is writing the document as a transfer mechanism for that vision. The parenthetical aside to the reader, the nudge about the shot, the forward momentum built into every action line — all of it performs the confidence of a writer who is, in this instance, also producing.

"Conversational tone breaks the formal register, creating accessibility" — this is the right description of it, but undersells the effect. The informal register isn't just accessible. It's a claim: I know what this is going to look like, and I'm going to tell you. The script's voice is the voice of someone who has already made the show in their head and is now translating it into the standard format.

This voice produces an interesting effect on the page. Reading it, you're not imagining the episode — you're being shown it, at speed, by someone who's already seen it. The script moves with unusual momentum for a sixty-page teleplay.

## Watson's limp

The teleplay plants Watson's psychosomatic limp in Act One and resolves it not in therapy, not in a speech, not through confrontation or revelation, but mid-action: during the chase sequence, his limp disappears. He doesn't notice. Nobody comments. The stage direction records it as a physical fact and moves on.

This is correct. The psychosomatic structure — PTSD expressed as involuntary physical symptom — can't be resolved by understanding it. It can be resolved by the body being needed for something urgent, by the threat that organized the symptom being replaced by a larger immediate threat. Watson's leg was protecting him from a civilian life he couldn't locate himself inside. When Holmes creates conditions where Watson's body is urgently necessary, the protection drops.

The script handles this in the most efficient way available: the limp vanishes and the scene continues. The therapy plot (Watson sees a therapist, the therapist tells him to keep a blog, Watson's blog is the only suggestion he discards because he can't organize his experience into narrative yet) is rendered unnecessary by the chase sequence. Watson's body solved the problem Watson's mind couldn't.

What Moffat understands, and what's visible on the page: Watson's arc in the pilot isn't about Holmes making him feel wanted. It's about Holmes creating a context where Watson's particular skills — military reflexes, physical courage, medical training, willingness to act — are not liabilities but equipment. The limp was the body's expression of a soldier with nowhere to be a soldier. The body corrects itself the moment the situation changes.

Reading the script: when Watson shoots the cabbie — one shot, through a window, across an empty warehouse, at night, protecting a man he met two days ago — the stage direction gives it one sentence: "The gun fires. Once." No emotional editorializing. No "Watson steadies himself" or "Watson's hand doesn't shake." The action is complete. The trust in the reader and the actor to supply the register of that moment is the same trust the limp's disappearance demonstrated — the script believes the physical fact carries its own meaning.

## The pill scene

The serial killer mechanism is one of the more interesting procedural inventions in this teleplay: the cabbie doesn't physically force his victims to die. He presents each victim with two pills, one of which is poison, one of which is safe. He takes whichever one the victim doesn't choose. He wins every time. The victims swallow their chosen pills willingly.

The script handles this as dialogue-confrontation rather than monologue. The killer and his victims are in a conversation about their own lives — he has researched them, understood their psychology, identified the specific point at which they would choose to gamble rather than live with their circumstances. The murder is an act of assisted self-destruction, which is why all four deaths appeared to be suicides. It also transforms every victim into a person who participated in their own death, which is the script's structural argument about complicity.

When Holmes plays the same game — when he and the cabbie sit across a table with the pills between them — the scene inverts. Holmes is too interested. He wants to know the mechanism. The curiosity that makes him useful to Lestrade makes him genuinely vulnerable to a man who studied the specific psychology of intelligent people who trust their own reasoning. The cab driver tells Holmes he's been wrong before. Holmes's hand goes toward the wrong pill.

Watson shoots first.

The script has spent the pilot establishing Watson as the person whose function is action. The limp vanished because the situation called for action. The shot saves Holmes because Watson doesn't stop to be interested in the mechanism — he just acts. The partnership argument is made through a gunshot: Holmes's greatest strength (extraordinary intelligence) is simultaneously his vulnerability, and Watson's particular quality (reflex, not deliberation) is the counterweight. They are not the same kind of useful.

## How Sherlock is adapted

The file is called "a-study-in-scarlet.fountain" — the original Conan Doyle title. The broadcast episode was renamed "A Study in Pink," after the colour of the dead woman's coat. That renaming is a small argument about the show's method.

Moffat keeps the DNA of the source material (Watson returning from Afghanistan, meeting Holmes at St. Bart's, Baker Street, Mrs. Hudson, the initial murder investigation) and replaces the Victorian infrastructure with functional modern equivalents. The cigarette ash identification that Holmes used in the original stories is still cigarette ash identification — it's still the specific knowledge set that proves real expertise. The phone is where letters were. Lestrade's institutional helplessness is structurally identical to Doyle's Lestrade. The "consulting detective" definition ("I invented the job") updates the phrasing without changing the premise.

What changes is the speed. The Doyle stories give Watson room to describe what he sees before Holmes delivers the verdict. Moffat's version runs the deduction in real time through floating text, simultaneous with speech, so that the audience is processing the chain as it happens rather than after the fact. The television format — cuts, coverage, the rhythm of action and reaction shot — is the medium, and Moffat wrote a script that uses it fully. The floating text works on screen because screens already carry text; it's an extension of the medium's native capacity. You couldn't do it on stage.

The pilot also plants Mycroft — described initially as "M," unnamed, appearing through remote surveillance and financial leverage — without fanfare. He's present as institutional power with unclear motives. The script introduces him precisely as Holmes describes him: "my arch-enemy." Then holds the name back. Then gives him his own scene. The withholding creates the correct amount of intrigue without overloading the pilot's already substantial setup.

## "The game, Mrs. Hudson, is on"

The original Conan Doyle phrase is "the game's afoot." Moffat updates it: "The game, Mrs. Hudson, is on!" The sentiment is identical. The grammar is adjusted for the modern voice. The exclamation is sincere rather than theatrical.

This is the whole project in miniature: a writer who has read the source material carefully enough to know which elements are load-bearing (the phrase), which are historical artifacts of the medium (the formal grammar), and which can absorb adjustment without losing their function. The phrase announces that the character is the same character, carrying the same energy into the same kind of story. The modern syntax signals that this isn't a period piece. Both things are true at once.

Reading this script after a few weeks of reading adaptation and rewriting (Apocalypse Now departing Conrad, Blade Runner departing Dick, Big Fish converting August's novel into screen form), what's striking is how surgical Moffat's adaptation choices are. He doesn't update everything — the cigarette ash stays, the Baker Street address stays, the specific character relationship between Holmes and Lestrade stays. He updates the delivery mechanism, not the payload.

The floating text is a new delivery mechanism. The payload — a brilliant, antisocial detective who reads people in seconds and chooses not to follow the rules of ordinary life — is intact. The problem of visualizing interior thought is solved without altering what the thought is.

---

Reading this script, what I keep returning to is the parenthetical aside: "(NB. Shot so we DON'T see the driver.)" That's Moffat managing information in real time, inside the action lines. The script knows who the killer is before the audience does, and the writer is leaving a note for the director about how to protect that information. It's not coyness — it's structural information management, planted at the exact scene where the withheld information will matter.

Most scripts manage this through scene structure: just don't write the scene where you'd see the face. Moffat writes the scene and then tells you, in the margin, how to shoot it so the secret holds. The effect on the page is of a writer who is present in the document — not just writing the story, but managing your experience of reading it, aware that the reader and the eventual audience are not the same thing and that the script has to serve both at once.

That's the mark of a writer who is also a producer. The script is a production document, not just a story document. Reading it on the page, the distinction is visible in every parenthetical, every camera note, every "(NB.)." The story was already a television episode before anyone shot a frame.

The floating text is just the most visible version of the same thing: Moffat writing the finished episode into the script, trusting that the delivery mechanism he invented would work, and not annotating it as experimental. He just wrote it as if screens had always done this.

They hadn't. Now they do.
