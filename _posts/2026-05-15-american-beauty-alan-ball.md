The camera descends into the neighborhood in the first line. "We're FLYING above suburban America, DESCENDING SLOWLY toward a tree-lined street." In the last scene, it rises again: "We're FLYING once again over Robin Hood Trail, ASCENDING SLOWLY." The camera movement is the structural bracket of the entire film, and Ball writes it as geography — down into life, up out of it — without a single word of commentary.

That's the argument in camera direction. The film's thesis isn't in the dialogue. It's in the movement.

---

## Dead already

In less than a year, I'll be dead.

That's line eleven. Before we've seen anything beyond the neighborhood. Before we know who's talking or why. Lester Burnham announces his own death before we've watched him live for more than a few seconds.

Reading this on the page, you feel the formal decision. Ball doesn't hide the ending or build toward it. He puts it in the first breath of narration and moves on. "Of course, I don't know that yet." Then: "And in a way, I'm dead already."

The effect is to eliminate one kind of dramatic tension — what happens? — and replace it with another. We know what happens. The question is what it means. And more specifically: what Lester is looking at in his last moment, what he thinks about it, whether that thought is true or sentimental or both.

Ball had this option because he invented a narrator who knows the ending. Lester Burnham is dead through the whole film. His V.O. is retrospective — he's already arrived at wherever you go after. That structural choice gives Ball a second film running under the first: not just Lester's suburban misery, but whatever Lester comes to understand before the bullet arrives.

The trick is that Ball hides when he switches between them. Most of the film is the first film. The last three pages are the second.

## The fantasy grammar

Reading the rose petal sequences, you notice that Ball wrote a specific technical grammar for the fantasies. Everything SLOWS DOWN. The MUSIC acquires an eerie ECHO. The other girls DISAPPEAR entirely. And then SMASH CUT back to reality. The same notation, the same sequence, every time.

The first one happens at the basketball game:

> His POV: Angela looks directly at us now, dancing only for Lester. Her movements take on a blatantly erotic edge as she starts to unzip her uniform, teasing us with an expression that's both innocent and knowing, then... she pulls her uniform OPEN and a profusion of RED ROSE PETALS spill forth... and we SMASH CUT TO:
>
> Angela, fully clothed, is once again surrounded by the other girls.

The cut is written into the stage direction. It's not a production note about editing — it's the rhythm of the scene, and the rhythm is the point. The fantasy is vivid, complete, physically specific. The reality is abrupt. The "SMASH CUT" is the joke and the critique simultaneously: here's what desire does to perception, and here's how fast it doesn't hold.

Ball uses the rose petals as connective tissue across the whole script. The film opens on a close-up of a rose. The fantasies put rose petals everywhere Angela appears. And the death shot — the moment the gun fires — cuts immediately to "an arrangement of fresh-cut ROSES in a vase on the opposite counter, deep crimson against the WHITE TILE WALL. Then a GUNSHOT suddenly rings out... instantly, the tile is sprayed with BLOOD, the same deep crimson as the roses."

Blood and roses, the same color. Ball has been building this rhyme from the first close-up of the film: AMERICAN BEAUTY ROSE. The title image is the death image. The fantasies were decorating the murder scene all along.

## The stage direction makes a judgment

Ball's action lines routinely editorialize. Not constantly, not obviously — but in the moments that matter, the stage directions stop describing and start judging.

On Ricky's introduction: "Ricky is eighteen, but his eyes are much older. Beneath his Zen-like tranquility lurks something wounded... and dangerous."

On the amusement park photograph: "It's startling how happy they look." (Said twice, once in Act One, once in Act Three — the same line, same photo, same judgment, placed so the repetition functions as an edit across 60 pages.)

On the Colonel, moments before he kisses Lester: "He opens his eyes and looks at Lester, his face filled with an anguished vulnerability we wouldn't have thought possible from him."

And on Angela, the moment the fantasy ends: "His POV: Angela lies beneath us, embarrassed and vulnerable. This is not the mythically carnal creature of Lester's fantasies; this is a nervous child."

That last one is the clearest. Ball writes it from the camera's perspective — "his POV" — and then immediately overrides the POV with the script's own evaluation. The camera belongs to Lester; the judgment belongs to Ball. This is the moment the film steps out of Lester's subjectivity and tells you what it actually thinks. The stage direction is doing something stage directions aren't supposed to do, and Ball does it exactly once, in exactly the right place.

## The Colonel's misread

The most precisely engineered scene in the script is the one where the Colonel watches Lester and Ricky through a rain-blurred garage window.

Ball describes what the Colonel sees: Ricky's back to the window, Lester leaning back in the chair, "his jaw drops, then he throws his head back." From the Colonel's perspective, through blurred glass, from a distance, it looks like oral sex.

Then Ball writes: "From our perspective, it looks very much like Ricky is giving Lester a blow job."

He tells you directly that the script is staging a misread. He acknowledges it explicitly. And he does it because the misread is the structure — the Colonel's suspicion is a confession. The only way to read a drug purchase as a sexual encounter is to already be looking for the sexual encounter. The Colonel's accusation tells us where his mind is.

That misread drives his confrontation with Ricky, which drives Ricky's liberation, which sends Ricky to the Burnham house, which leaves the Colonel alone. Which leads to the garage, the kiss, the humiliation, the gun.

Ball built the entire third act on a staging choice — what a man sees through a rain-blurred window — and he made sure to tell the reader, in the stage direction, that the misread is deliberate. He needed the reader to be in on the construction.

## The kiss

The Colonel's garage scene is extraordinary for what it doesn't do.

He arrives at the Burnham garage, drenched, disoriented. Lester pulls him in, jokes about Carolyn, puts his hand on the Colonel's shoulder. "We really should get you out of these clothes." The Colonel whispers "Yes..."

Then:

> He opens his eyes and looks at Lester, his face filled with an anguished vulnerability we wouldn't have thought possible from him. His eyes are brimming with tears. Lester leans in, concerned.
>
> "It's okay."
>
> "I..."
>
> "Just tell me what you need."
>
> The Colonel reaches up and places his hand on Lester's cheek... and then kisses him. Lester is momentarily stunned, and then he pushes the Colonel away. The Colonel's face crumples in shame.
>
> "Whoa, whoa, whoa. I'm sorry. You got the wrong idea."
>
> The Colonel stares at the floor, blinking, and then he turns and runs out the open garage door into the rainy night.

Lester's response is "you got the wrong idea." Not mockery. Not disgust. Not triumph. The same warmth he's been building toward throughout his transformation, applied to the man who's been terrorizing his neighborhood. It's the kindest thing Lester does in the film.

The Colonel runs. That's the whole scene. Ball doesn't linger, doesn't annotate, doesn't explain. He cuts immediately to Carolyn in the Mercedes. The scene ends on a man running into the rain from his own desire.

## Angela and the sandwich

The fantasy of Angela and the reality of Angela are the same person, which Ball has been careful to obscure for the first ninety pages and then reveal in two scenes.

The virginity disclosure happens mid-act, on a couch with Angela's blouse open. Ball writes Lester's realization: "His POV: Angela lies beneath us, embarrassed and vulnerable. This is not the mythically carnal creature of Lester's fantasies; this is a nervous child." Lester laughs at her announcement — not cruelly, but as recognition, the laugh of someone who realizes the joke is on him. He wraps her in a blanket. He hugs her while she cries. "You have nothing to be sorry about."

Then they go to the kitchen and Angela eats a turkey sandwich.

"Angela is sprawled across the counter. She's eating a turkey sandwich." "Wow. I was starving."

That's it. The formal resolution of the desire that's driven the whole film is a teenager eating a sandwich in a kitchen at night, admitted to being hungry, slightly weirded out but basically okay. Lester makes her a sandwich. They talk about Jane.

Ball earns this scene by not trying to make it into anything. No symbolic weight, no ironic commentary, no closing of the fantasy with a formal antithesis. Just food, because the girl was hungry. The rose petals don't appear here. There's nothing to cut back from. This is already the real world.

## Carolyn's slap

Carolyn has a tell. She slaps herself when she starts to cry — once in the sale house, once in the Top Hat Motel parking lot after Buddy ends the affair. "As before, she SLAPS herself, hard." Ball writes "as before" the second time, making the repetition explicit. She also slaps Jane. "Suddenly she SLAPS Jane, hard."

The same gesture, directed inward and outward. Carolyn cannot tolerate softness — in herself, in her daughter, in anyone who exposes her to it. "You cannot count on anyone except yourself." She says this to Jane while crying, which is the most Carolyn moment in the script. The thesis and the violation of the thesis in the same breath.

Reading all the Carolyn scenes as a sequence, you notice that Ball has built her entire character around what she prevents herself from feeling. She ends the film clutching Lester's clothes in the closet, burying her face in them — the smell of someone she couldn't allow herself to stay married to. She arrives at the house with a gun in her purse and hides the gun in a hamper. She was going to kill him. She decided not to. She didn't get to make the choice that would have destroyed her because someone else made a different choice first.

That irony isn't underlined anywhere in the script. Ball just builds both threads in parallel — Carolyn loading the gun, Carolyn deciding not to use it, the Colonel loading a different gun — and lets the reader sit with what it means.

---

## The last seconds

Lester sits at the kitchen table with the photograph. He's been on the journey Ball set up in the first lines — he found what he was looking for, the sense of being alive again, even if what he found wasn't what he thought he was looking for. Angela ate a sandwich. Jane is upstairs with Ricky. Carolyn is parking outside.

He's looking at the photograph of his family at an amusement park. "It's startling how happy they look." The third time this image appears in the script, it's the last thing Lester sees.

The gun appears behind his head. Ball cuts immediately to the roses — not to the man shot, but to the flowers, and then the wall becomes the same color as the flowers. The script handles the murder in two images. The actual death is the cutting rhythm, not the shot.

Then the ascending camera, the V.O. over black-and-white images from childhood, and Lester's last words directed at the audience:

> I guess I could be pretty pissed off about what happened to me... but it's hard to stay mad, when there's so much beauty in the world.

> You have no idea what I'm talking about, I'm sure. But don't worry... You will someday.

Whether you believe that is the film's whole question, left completely open. Ball spent a hundred pages building evidence for both sides. Lester was right that he was dead already. He was also right that something changed. Whether what changed was worth what it cost everyone around him is the conversation the script refuses to have.

The camera ascends. The neighborhood gets smaller. The last line of the script is spoken in darkness, after the FADE TO BLACK. "You will someday." A promise from a dead man to an audience that hasn't died yet.

Ball bets everything on that being moving rather than sentimental. Reading it on the page, having followed all the structural work — the roses, the misread, the sandwich, the slapping, the descending camera a hundred pages ago — I think he wins the bet.

Just barely. That's the right margin for this kind of ending.
