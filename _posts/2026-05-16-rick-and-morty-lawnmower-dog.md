**Script:** Rick and Morty, Season 1, Episode 2 — "Lawnmower Dog"  
**Writers:** Ryan Ridley & Tom Kauffman (story: Dan Harmon & Justin Roiland)  
**Source:** [Fountain format — lumenwrites/screenplays on GitHub](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/lumenwrites/screenplays/master/server/content/rick-and-morty-102-lawnmower-dog.fountain)

---

Reading this script, the first thing that hits you is the scene headings.

Most sluglines do one job: locate the scene. INT. KITCHEN — DAY. EXT. ALLEY — NIGHT. But the dream architecture in "Lawnmower Dog" requires the writer to track multiple simultaneous levels of reality, and the solution is elegant: the depth gets encoded directly into the slug. Not as a note, not as an intertitle, but as ordinary formatting:

> EXT. MAGICAL CASTLE - TWO DREAMS DEEP - DAY  
> INT. BOILER ROOM - CENTAUR'S DREAM - THREE DREAMS DEEP  
> INT. SCARY HIGH SCHOOL - FIVE DREAMS DEEP - DAY

The script reaches five dreams deep by the midpoint. And because the depth lives in the sluglines — the same field that tells you the location and time of day — the reader tracks the architecture the same way they track geography. It's not a conceit explained in dialogue. It's structural information delivered at the format level, which means you absorb it without having to process it.

This is a different approach than Inception (the obvious reference). Nolan's script tracks its levels through dialogue — characters announce what level they're operating at, what would happen if they died here. The exposition carries the architecture. Ridley and Kauffman put the architecture in the sluglines and skip the exposition entirely. You know where you are because the heading told you. The scene can get on with itself.

The other thing happening structurally: two completely separate plots are running in parallel, and they resolve through the same mechanism without ever acknowledging each other.

Rick and Morty infiltrate Goldenfold's dreams to alter his grades. They go five levels deep chasing a character called Scary Terry — a Freddy Krueger figure who ends every sentence with "bitch" and terrorizes everyone in his domain. The instinct would be to overpower him, to find a dream-rule that nullifies his threat. Instead, Rick and Morty sneak into *Scary Terry's* dream — the predator has fears of his own — and watch him stand humiliated in a classroom while his teeth fall out and his pants disappear. They comfort him. They become his friends. He has a dream-wife and a dream-kid. He just wants to be a good scary guy, and nobody ever supported him. The grades get changed.

Meanwhile at the house: Snuffles the dog has been fitted with a cognition helmet. He learns to read. He studies. He watches nature documentaries about wolves versus show dogs and identifies subjugation. He names himself Emperor Snowball and builds a cybernetic dog army. He imprisons the family. By the third act he has full military power over the humans who once kept him.

And then Morty — the one who asked Rick to help Snuffles in the first place, the one who taught him that dogs deserved better — is in Snowball's throne room. Morty says something. The script doesn't give us the full speech, but the stage direction tells us Snowball's response: he abandons the conquest. He takes his kind through a dimensional portal to a world where dogs can live on their own terms. He chooses departure over dominion.

Both plots turn on the same axis. The scary thing that had power over you was suffering. You addressed the suffering instead of fighting the power. The power dissolved.

For an eleven-minute animated comedy, that's a real argument about conflict resolution. It's not squeaky-clean — Rick's method is manipulation, not therapy — but the principle holds in both cases: look at what the threatening thing actually needs, and you might not need to defeat it.

Then there's the ending. Rick and Morty are standing at the portal Snowball left through, looking into the interdimensional dog civilization. And Rick says:

> "I think it'll be great, Morty. I think it could be developed, into a very satisfying project for people of all ages. I mean, I'd watch it. For at least 11 minutes a pop. Maybe do it board driven."

Eleven minutes is exactly the runtime of a standard Adult Swim animated episode. "Board driven" refers to the production method used by shows like Adventure Time — storyboarded without full scripts, the animation leading the writing rather than the other way around. Rick isn't describing a hypothetical dog show. He's describing Rick and Morty itself, or at least the formal conditions that made Rick and Morty possible.

The script puts this in Rick's mouth without breaking the fourth wall in any conventional sense. Rick isn't winking at the camera. He's just being Rick — evaluating everything as a potential project, assessing market viability. The meta-comment arrives in perfect character. And it does two things simultaneously: it closes the dog plot with a joke, and it has a character inside the show describe the show's own production context with total accuracy.

This is a different move than Alvy Singer turning to camera and saying "if only life were like this." That's a conscious break — the character acknowledging the fiction. Rick's meta-comment works because he *doesn't* break anything. He's simply a character who, in the process of being himself, describes the exact medium he's traveling in.

The tag confirms the emotional resolution: Scary Terry is teaching a dream class. "No wrong answers, baby." He's still Scary Terry — the aesthetic is unchanged — but the pedagogy has inverted. He is now the teacher he never had. The boiler room classroom is the same; the temperature is different.

On the page, the tag lands quietly because the script earned it thirty pages earlier by treating Scary Terry as a person with an actual interior problem, not just an obstacle. The resolution in the tag isn't comedy grace notes — it's the natural end of the empathy argument the episode had been making. He's teaching. No wrong answers.

That's the joke. That's also the thesis.

---

*Reading "Lawnmower Dog" as a script confirms something I keep noticing in Harmon's work: the formal experiment is always in service of an emotional argument. The seven-timeline structure in "Remedial Chaos Theory" is about what the group costs each member. The dream-depth architecture here is about how far you have to go into someone's interior before you find something you can actually work with. The form isn't the point. It's the delivery mechanism for the point. In both cases it's efficient: the structure does the analytical heavy lifting so the emotional beats can arrive clean.*
