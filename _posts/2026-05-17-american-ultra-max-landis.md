---

Reading this script, the first thing that hits is the stage direction talking back at you.

Not editorially — not the occasional judgment Alan Ball delivers in a single sentence, not the directorial prose Moffat sustains over a hundred pages of Sherlock. Landis keeps a running conversation going inside the action lines, with the reader, mid-scene, about what he's doing and why. When the Walmart fight reaches maximum density he writes: *"WOW, this is a lot of action to write out, let's hope you're having more fun reading it than if I just wrote 'Mike goes ape on these motherfuckers.'"* And then: *"This isn't a comic book. We all know ultimately it's going to come down to what we can shoot on the day anyway, right? So just take my word for it that the fight between Mike and Laugher makes any of the action in the new James Bond films look like fucking 1960s Batman."*

He's naming the genre mid-script. Declaring intent and then performing it. The genre declaration is "Stoner Bourne, not Kafka" — delivered as a casual aside about keeping the page count down. And this isn't just charm. It's a contract. The script announces what it's going to do and then does it. The Walmart sequence is genuinely written aisle by aisle with household weapons — Raid, pitchfork, garden shears, rubber mallet against heads popping up like whack-a-mole — because Landis describes the emotional logic of the action rather than the footwork, hands it to a choreographer with trust, and moves on. The deflection is the craft decision.

I've been tracking Landis's stage direction narrator since Dirk Gently, where the narrator makes promises and keeps them. In American Ultra the narrator does something more specifically calibrated: it manages reader expectation in real time. When Mike kills two men with a spoon in the grocery parking lot and yells *"What the FUCK just happened?!"* — that's also the reader's question. The script is always one beat ahead of the audience's bewilderment, either confirming it or getting there first.

---

The script's deepest structural move is the "I am a tree" speech.

Mike, crying in Phoebe's car after the failed Hawaii trip, explaining why he's ruining her life: *"I am a tree. Am I that tree and you're that car and I'm just stopping you and the guy goes through the windshield?"* He's hysterical. Phoebe is trying not to laugh. It's the script's funniest scene in the first act.

But Mike doesn't know that he was literally engineered to be unmovable. The anxieties, the panic attacks, the inability to leave Liman, Oregon — all of it was installed. Lasseter tells him later: *"The anxieties, the phobias, we did that to you. To protect you."* The CIA turned him into an anchor on purpose. His metaphor is more accurate than he knows. The script's darkest structural joke: a man weeping that he might be a tree is literally weeping about being a tree, and neither he nor the audience has the information to understand that yet.

That's not a twist. It's a plant with a thirty-page delayed detonation.

---

The Phoebe reveal is the script's biggest structural event and Landis plants it carefully, mostly through her job.

She's a bail bondsman. Not a CIA cover chosen arbitrarily — the perfect operational position for someone managing a repeat-offender asset. When Mike kills two men in the parking lot and calls her ("I just killed two dudes!"), she has the professional infrastructure to show up, assess the scene, and pivot. Sheriff Watts knows her. She's managed Mike's legal trouble for years. Every piece of the cover is load-bearing.

The reveal lands at maximum emotional investment: right after she talked him through the Rose house chaos via phone (*"you CAN do this, Mike, I believe in you"*), right after he's vomited Riofloxin K into the ravine. He's pallid. He's shaking. He asks why she knows what the gas was. She says: *"Mike...I'm CIA, okay, I—"* and he just screams.

What Landis does next is the important thing. She doesn't deny anything. She gives him the truth: *"My last name is Selburg, Phoebe is my real name."* The first and clearest fact, delivered without spin. She's a spy who doesn't run a counter-argument. The information is her defense. And Lasseter fills in the rest later: Phoebe was supposed to leave. She stayed. She gave up her real life. *"I've actually never seen this in undercover work."*

The tragedy is that the information Mike most needs — the thing that would actually answer his question — is the thing Phoebe can't tell him without destroying what they have. So she just tells him her last name, and hopes it's enough.

---

The engagement ring is in the script from page thirty and survives everything that happens to the plot.

Mike had it before Hawaii. He's carrying it through every action sequence. It surfaces twice in the quiet moments — once in the jail cell, once in the car while Phoebe's confessing — and both times Mike puts it away without using it. The script is patient with it. It waits.

The proposal happens in front of Seal Team 6. Phoebe is covered in blood. Mike is on one knee in a parking lot of military vehicles and Black Hawk helicopters. He says: *"Are you want to...marryme?"* She cries, wipes blood off her face, says yes. Mike yells *"SHE SAID YES! WOOO—"* and then takes about a million Tasers and drops instantly.

*!SLAM TO BLACK.*

I haven't read a proposal scene that earns this specific kind of absurdity without cheapening what it is. The ring means something the entire script — it's the one thing Mike chose, that nobody installed in him, the artifact of genuine intention. When Phoebe says yes in this specific moment, the comedy and the emotion are doing the same work. Love persisting through maximum escalation isn't the joke. The Tasers are the joke. The yes is real.

---

Yates is the script's most technically demanding character to write because he has to be both genuinely funny and genuinely threatening — and the same speech has to do both.

The barrel speech is the proof: *"She's trying to put me over a barrel, here — put you over a barrel, it's an idiom, it means you fuck someone over, someone bent over a barrel, their ass is in the air, they get fucked in the ass. That's what she's trying to do is fuck my ass. [...] You make a report to central for insubordination, we FUCK HER IN THE ASS. She thinks she can put me over a barrel, no, fuck her, BIGGER BARREL, I'm taking it up the line, I got barrels for fuckin days!"*

This is a man who has organized all institutional conflict into the grammar of sexual domination, and is doing it at full volume, in a government office, to a subordinate who is trying not to look at him. The speech is funny. It's also diagnostic. Yates isn't evil so much as he's someone who got authority before his personality was ready to carry it, and is now doing everything at max volume hoping it works.

And Phoebe's realization in the command tent — *"Phoebe realizes he's insane. She looks to Otis, like 'Can you believe this?' Otis looks at her hard, emotionless, and Phoebe realizes just how fucked she is."* — lands because the script has maintained both registers simultaneously. The comedy and the danger are the same object.

---

Rocket Monkey and Chip the Brick.

Mike draws them constantly. A monkey astronaut and a sentient brick, going on adventures that he sketches on the back of whatever paper's available, that he works on while ringing up groceries, that pile up on the floor of Phoebe's house in stacks.

The whole script is a revelation of what's fake: his name, his job, his inability to leave town, his girlfriend's identity. Everything he thought was real is constructed. But nobody installed Rocket Monkey. Nobody told him to draw a monkey astronaut. He made that up himself, from nothing, in the years between being a government asset and being a stoner in Oregon, and it's the only thing in his life that's entirely his.

Phoebe uses them to keep him alive in the ravine, the one thing she can reach when the gas is shutting his senses down: *"If you die, what about Rocket Monkey, Mike? What about Chip the Brick? If you die what happens to all your stories they disappear, right? You never even got to write them down!"*

It works. He vomits the gas and survives.

The after-credits scene: Mike in an industrial kitchen, surrounded by international criminals, and the only thing he had on him was a napkin. The script doesn't say what he drew. But you already know.

---

The coldest scene in the script has no action and barely any comedy.

Krueger shoots Yates in a snowy field, three times in the chest, mid-sentence, and then stands over him and explains exactly what crime Yates committed and why the paperwork was too annoying to file. Then he turns to Lasseter — shaking, terrified — and waits.

She doesn't make a human being argument. She makes the institutional one: *"Mike Howell dismantled the entire Toughguy program singlehandedly. That makes him the most effective asset ANY of the Ultra programs have ever seen. In sixty years. [...] That is a puppy worth saving. That is a two hundred million dollar puppy."*

Krueger clicks off the timer on his watch. The implication is clear: she made it in time.

What's devastating about this is that she's right on both axes simultaneously. Mike is worth saving because he's a human being who didn't choose any of this. Mike is also worth saving because of an asset valuation. Lasseter knows exactly which argument to make and makes it without apology, because she's in the business of institutional survival and there's a man's life on the line.

The script doesn't comment on this. Yates lies in the snow between them. The timer stops.

---

On the page, American Ultra reads as a genre script that's been inhabited so thoroughly by its writer's voice that the genre becomes secondary. The "Stoner Bourne" declaration is accurate but it's not the thesis. The thesis is the ring. The thesis is Rocket Monkey. The thesis is Phoebe saying "my last name is Selburg" and holding the gaze and not running.

Everything in Mike's life was built around him without his knowledge. Everything that turns out to be worth anything — the drawings, the proposal, the girlfriend who stayed when she wasn't supposed to — he made himself, in the gap between what the program installed and what a person does when left alone with time.

The script lets him keep those things at the end. That's not sentiment. It's the argument.
