---

Reading this script, the first thing that strikes you is what the scene headings are doing.

Every motel room scene carries a tag: `##BLACK AND WHITE SEQUENCE##`. Every color scene carries `<colour sequence="">`. These aren't production notes appended at the bottom. They're in the scene heading itself, before anything else — before the location, before the time of day. The reader knows what kind of consciousness they're entering before the scene begins.

This is not a formatting convention. Nolan invented it for this script because the script needed it. And what it does, structurally, is encode the film's temporal logic directly into the one thing the reader encounters first on every scene: the heading. The heading is the reader's equivalent of Leonard's tattoos. It tells you where you are before you can orient yourself through the content.

---

The opening is worth looking at carefully because it encodes the film's method in a single mechanical action.

Scene 1: a Polaroid photograph of a dead body. The image in the photo starts to fade. A hand fans it rapidly — trying to force the chemistry. The image fades to nothing. The hand places the blank photo back at the front of the camera. The camera sucks the picture up. The flash bursts.

The Polaroid is being run backward. Not metaphorically — physically. The hand fans the photo trying to reverse its development, gives up, and returns it to the camera before the picture was taken. The scene ends with the murder happening in reverse: blood leaping off Leonard's face and suit and reassembling itself into Teddy's living face.

Reading this on the page, it works as scene geometry before you understand why. You're watching Leonard try to manipulate photographic chemistry by brute will — and it doesn't work. Polaroids develop forward, not back. The fanning is futile against the chemistry. What the script is telling you in its first scene is that the investigation running backward through the film is also futile against the chemistry of what happened. The truth won't develop from fanning.

---

The voiceover isn't narration. That's the thing to understand about how Nolan uses it.

Conventional V.O. knows more than the scene and explains it to us. Leonard's V.O. is running below his own consciousness level — he's narrating his current situation to himself as though he were a stranger arriving into it. "So where are you?" He opens scene 2 with that question, asking it about himself, in second person, while lying in a motel bed.

This is the cognitive prosthesis made audible. Leonard can't trust internal continuity, so he constructs it externally, as narration. He interviews himself about his location. He narrates his note-taking process as he executes it. The V.O. isn't there to explain to the audience — it's there because it's happening: Leonard is actually doing this, continuously, to maintain himself.

The result is that the V.O. feels less like access and more like observation. You're watching someone manage their own self-assembly in real time. The Sammy Jankis parallel — which Leonard draws through extended V.O. while investigating — isn't backstory. It's Leonard thinking by comparison, building his own case from someone else's case file. He constructs his identity through analogy because he can't build it from memory.

---

The script's structural logic is that scenes end at information thresholds, not action ones.

Scene 57, set in Natalie's apartment: things happen (dinner, conversation, sex), but the scene's ending isn't the sex or Leonard leaving. The scene ends when Leonard writes on Natalie's Polaroid: "DON'T TRUST HER." That's the cut point. The scene's terminal event is documentation. Leonard transforming experience into a recorded fact.

This runs consistently through the script. Scenes close when Leonard's knowledge of the scene crosses into his external record of it. The cut is the moment of inscription — tattoo, Polaroid note, written card. Experience isn't complete, for Leonard, until it's archived. The script's cutting logic performs his condition rather than just depicting it.

---

One stage direction that stayed with me: "Teddy, worried, affects a casual air, shrugging dismissively."

The word "affects" is doing specific work. Not "assumes" a casual air, not "projects" one — *affects*. A word that signals performance known to the actor as performance. Nolan's script, in a story about memory manipulation and the uncertainty of what any given account can be trusted to mean, uses a word in the action line that flags Teddy as performing before any dialogue reveals whether his account is true.

In most scripts, stage direction vocabulary just needs to be clear. Here, the word choice is part of the argument. "Affects" tells the reader what the scene can't resolve: that Teddy is managing an impression, consciously. The script can't tell you whether Teddy is lying — that remains unresolved — but it can tell you that he's performing. The distinction matters.

---

The final structural piece is the one that, on the page, feels most completely thought-through: the incompatibility of the two evidentiary tracks.

Leonard's documented record — tattoos, Polaroid annotations, written notes — and the verbal claims of Teddy and Natalie exist side by side through the whole script and are never adjudicated. The script provides no third perspective capable of telling you which set of facts is reliable. When Teddy asserts in scene 115 that everything Leonard has documented is wrong, and physically takes control of Leonard's photographs while making the assertion, the script just shows you both: the documented fact and the person denying the documentation. Then it cuts.

Reading this draft, you're in exactly Leonard's epistemic position. You have the scene headings, the action lines, the V.O. — all presented as neutral fact — and you have characters making claims that undercut the factual record. The same information asymmetry, from inside it.

That's not a trick. It's a formal argument about what memory-based selfhood consists of: a set of records you can't verify against a continuous experience that no longer exists, surrounded by people with their own reasons to tell you what you are.

The pink draft doesn't resolve this. The finished film didn't either. The fanning continues.
