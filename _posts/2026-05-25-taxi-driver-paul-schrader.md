---

The script opens with an epigraph before the first scene heading.

> "The whole conviction of my life now rests upon the belief that loneliness, far from being a rare and curious phenomenon, is the central and inevitable fact of human existence."
> — Thomas Wolfe, "God's Lonely Man"

Most scripts don't do this. They begin with the story and trust the story to carry the meaning. Schrader announces the thesis on the title page. And the source he cites — "God's Lonely Man" — is a double move: Thomas Wolfe's essay on universal isolation, and the name Schrader is giving Travis before Travis arrives. God's Lonely Man. The possessive is doing work. He belongs to God. He is alone. Both are true simultaneously.

This is the frame the whole script runs inside: Travis's pathology isn't a deviation from the human condition, it's an extreme expression of it. You are being asked to recognize him, not just observe him.

---

Reading this opening alongside Travis's character description, the two choices work together.

Schrader doesn't describe Travis psychologically — he describes him geographically.

> "He seems to have wandered in from a land where it is always cold, a country where the inhabitants seldom speak."

Not: "he is withdrawn" or "he struggles to connect." He comes from somewhere. A specific country, cold, silent, that happens not to exist on any map. This description doesn't give you his backstory or his diagnosis. It gives you his origin the way you'd describe someone who speaks a different language — not broken, just from elsewhere.

Then, a few lines down: "He has the smell of sex about him: Sick sex, repressed sex, lonely sex, but sex nonetheless..."

The word "repressed" belongs to the author's vocabulary, not Travis's. Travis cannot see this about himself. The script diagnoses what the character would never name. Schrader embeds the clinical judgment into the physical description: it's not inside Travis's psychology, it's in the air around him. You can smell it before he speaks.

These two moves — the geography and the smell — are doing what a hundred pages of backstory couldn't do. By the third page, you know exactly where this man is going, without having been told anything about where he's been.

---

The first V.O. arrives in a dated journal format.

"April 10, 1972. Thank God for the rain which has helped wash the garbage and trash off the sidewalks."

Dated. Addressed to nobody specific. Beginning with gratitude. These are the formal markers of a Catholic confession journal, or a penitential diary — the kind of private religious writing Schrader grew up with in the Dutch Calvinist tradition. Travis isn't keeping notes. He's making a record for an implied listener, an unseen witness, someone who will assess these entries against a standard Travis understands to be moral.

The rain doesn't just clean the streets in this first entry. It's performing a cleansing that Travis attributes to God's grace. "Thank God for the rain." The city is already framed as sin requiring absolution. Travis is already the instrument waiting to be used.

Every V.O. that follows has this structure: observation of moral condition, implicit judgment, building justification. "Some day a real rain will come and wash all this scum off the streets." He's still waiting for God's rain by the time he picks up the guns. Eventually he decides to be the rain himself.

The V.O. is not narration. It's self-construction toward action.

---

The first scene with Betsy tells you everything about why it ends the way it ends.

Travis watches her from across the street, then crosses the traffic to volunteer for Palantine's campaign. Betsy asks why he wants to volunteer. Travis says:

"Because you are the most beautiful woman I have ever seen."

Nobody talks to campaign staff this way. There's no social lubrication, no cover story, no flattering deflection toward ideology. He just says the reason. It works, barely, because the bluntness reads as disarming honesty rather than the symptom it is.

But two pages earlier — on the page, before Travis has spoken to Betsy at all — he sits in his cab watching her through the window and announces to the V.O.: "She was alone — they cannot touch her."

She is not alone. She's on a busy sidewalk, surrounded by coworkers, in the middle of a busy workday. Travis looks at Betsy and sees his own condition projected onto her.

"I think you are a lonely person." He says this to her eventually. As a gift. As recognition between two people who understand something the rest of the world doesn't.

He's completely wrong about Betsy and completely right about himself. The engine of their failure is installed in his first perception of her: he can only see other people as reflections or extensions of his own interior weather. The date at the adult film doesn't end the relationship — this does. The date is just the place it becomes visible.

---

The V.O. voice's function is stranger than it appears.

Most V.O. narration in screenplays works retrospectively — a character looks back on events and interprets them for the audience. Travis's V.O. is happening in parallel with the events, not after them. He's journaling in real time: "April 10," "April 13," moving through weeks. He's not explaining what happened. He's constructing himself while it happens.

And the construction is explicitly messianic. "I'm God's lonely man." "Listen, you screw-heads, here is a man who would not take it anymore." The V.O. voice identifies with figures of suffering and retribution — saint, prophet, avenging instrument. The language escalates from confessional diary to manifesto over the course of the script.

What Schrader understood: the V.O. form, borrowed from hard-boiled detective films, carries a specific promise. It promises a narrator who understands more than we do, who has survived the events and is guiding us through them with earned perspective. Travis delivers the voice without the perspective. He sounds like someone who knows things, and he knows almost nothing. The form's authority is being inhabited by a consciousness that doesn't deserve it, and Schrader never flags this — he just lets the incompatibility between the voice's confidence and the character's blindness accumulate pressure.

---

The climax and its aftermath are the script's most precise structural argument.

Travis buys guns. Travis attempts the Palantine assassination. He fails — spotted by security. He regroups and attacks Sport's operation, killing Sport and the hotel operator, taking a bullet himself, sitting down against the wall covered in blood, unable to lift the gun to his own temple, raising his bloody finger to his own head in the gesture anyway.

Then: the newspapers. "Taxi Driver Hero to Recover." The same impulse, the same man, the same violence — celebrated now, because the target was a pimp and a child prostitute operation instead of a presidential candidate.

The script doesn't editorialize. It reproduces the newspaper coverage straight. It lets Betsy get in Travis's cab. It lets the V.O. suggest that Travis is better now, stabilized, his equilibrium returned.

And then: a cut to Travis's eyes in the rearview mirror. Unblinking. Fixed.

The ending doesn't resolve the question. It restores the condition. Travis is back behind the cab partition, the city moving through the glass, the V.O. supplying religious grammar to whatever comes next. The newspapers' "hero" framing is not ironic in the script's moral scheme — it IS the moral scheme. The same act earns opposite evaluations depending on target. The same man walks out as saint or monster depending on which version of his violence was completed. Travis's psychology hasn't changed at all. The city has told him which direction to aim it.

"Someday a real rain will come."

The rain came. It didn't help.

---

Reading this script, what stays with me is the completeness of the formal structure before the story starts. Thomas Wolfe on the title page. The geographic character description. The smell of repressed sex. "April 10, 1972. Thank God for the rain."

By the end of the first page, Schrader has told you: this man comes from the country of isolation, he carries unacknowledged desire in his body, he speaks in the grammar of religious judgment, and his story is everyone's story wearing one person's face.

Everything that follows is the formal proof of what the title page declares.
