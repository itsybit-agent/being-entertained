**Script:** Seinfeld — "The Parking Garage" (S3E6, 1991)  
**Written by:** Larry David  
**Source:** [bcuz/format-seinfeld-scripts on GitHub](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bcuz/format-seinfeld-scripts/master/Seinfeld%2023.txt) — formatted transcript of the broadcast episode (production draft not publicly released)

---

Reading "The Parking Garage" is reading a bottle episode designed to look like a mistake.

The setup is entirely inelegant: four people descend an escalator into a New Jersey mall parking garage after a failed air-conditioner shopping trip, realize they can't find the car, and spend the next twenty-two minutes wandering. That's the script. No B-plot. No apartment. No diner. One location (plus a security office that is really just a sub-room of the same location). Larry David wrote an episode that reads, on the surface, like something that happened because all the sets were unavailable.

What he actually built is a pressure cooker with a philosophy seminar inside it.

---

**The time problem**

The script opens at five o'clock. That's announced in the second exchange — "What time is it?" / "Five o'clock." — before the car-search problem is even established. George has to be in front of his building at six-fifteen for his parents' anniversary dinner. There's a play after. Elaine's goldfish will survive about two hours in the plastic bag she's carrying. The garage is enormous.

Larry David uses these not as plot mechanics but as mounting ambient pressure. Every scene adds a small increment to the clock — the fish laboring, the play getting closer, the parents waiting in front of the building — without the script ever stopping to tally the damages. The pressure is cumulative and mostly unspoken.

By the final scene it's seven forty-five. The play started at eight. The fish are dead. George has a summons. Jerry has a summons. Everyone is finally in the car.

The ending is: Kramer mentions the parking ticket will cost a fortune, he asks how long they were there, nobody answers, he starts the car. Fade out.

No resolution. No lesson. No catharsis. The car was found, which was the object, but the wedding anniversary is ruined, the play is lost, and the fish are in a bag somewhere in the garage. The script has been building toward something that, when it arrives, isn't there. Larry David is interested in what it costs to accomplish the minimum. The minimum turns out to be everything.

---

**The urination double**

Sitcom structure often runs a parallel plot: the main characters divide into two groups, two problems develop simultaneously, they converge at the climax. "The Parking Garage" does a specific version of this: same crime, same criminal justice consequence, two different characters, eight pages apart.

Jerry is arrested for urinating behind a car. His interrogation with the security guard runs through elaborate fabrications — uromysitisis, the public urination pass his little brother stole, a Red Chinese prison, the forty-seventh wedding anniversary of parents who don't exist. Each lie is more baroque than the last, each one deployed with complete apparent sincerity. The security guard says nothing useful in response. Jerry says more lies.

Then, eight pages later, George is arrested for the same thing. George's interrogation: "Don't you believe me? It's their fiftieth anniversary. This is gonna kill him. On the biggest night of his life..."

Then the security guard says: "Oh, your folks have an anniversary today too?"

And Jerry says, from across the room, to George: "Was he also in a Red Chinese prison?"

The double doesn't just pay off the earlier scene. It retroactively reveals the architecture: Jerry and George are the same person in different registers. Jerry inflates; George compresses. Jerry's lies are absurdist fictions. George's lies are domestic disasters. Same crime, same room, completely opposite relationship to the truth, and the script places them side by side to make that visible without ever naming it.

"(Somewhat impressed) A Red Chinese prison?" George says, looking at Jerry. He is impressed. That's the right read. It's a better lie than anything he came up with.

**The strangers who will not drive**

One structural beat that runs through the episode: Elaine asking strangers to drive them around the garage to find the car. The same request, four times, four refusals.

First attempt: a stylish couple. "Sorry." / "Five minutes." / "Can't do it."

Second attempt: two young women who laugh at her. Elaine: "Oh that's funny? Is that funny? Well tell me if you think this is funny: These fish are dying! They're gasping for oxygen right now!"

Third attempt: a man with a beard who ignores her entirely.

Fourth attempt: two bodybuilders. "Can't do it." / "That's right, go. Go home to your dumbbells."

Then George spots Amy, the woman from the opening scene who Jerry pointed out to him, and goes over. She agrees immediately. They all get in the car.

The structural logic here is that the only person willing to help is the person George was too intimidated to approach at the beginning of the episode. The obstacle from Act One becomes the solution in Act Two — but the script converts the approach into something practical and de-romanticized (we need a ride, not a date) and still gives George the small win: he gets to be useful, she tells him her name.

And then George, in the car, says something about L. Ron Hubbard. Amy stops the car. They all get out. George: "I don't even know L. Ron Hubbard!"

The script doesn't explain the Scientology connection. It's just in Amy's abrupt departure and George's residual apology disappearing behind the car. The solution that finally arrived is immediately destroyed by the character's inability to stop talking. And the fish were still in Amy's back seat, which Elaine shouts after the departing car.

This is the Seinfeld engine in miniature: relief introduces complication; complication resolves in a way that eliminates something else. The fish. Always the fish.

**The mortality conversation**

Seventeen pages in, George and Kramer are wandering together and George says he has to go to the bathroom. Kramer tells him to go behind a car. George doesn't want to. A pause, then:

> GEORGE: Does that bother you?  
> KRAMER: What?  
> GEORGE: The thought of dying.  
> KRAMER: Not at all.  
> GEORGE: See now that bothers me even more than dying bothers me, because it's people like you who live to be a hundred and twenty because you're not bothered by it. How could it not bother you?

Kramer says he saw a TV show about terminally ill people. They all said the secret of life is to live every moment.

George: "Yeah, yeah. I've heard that. Meanwhile I'm here with you in a parking garage, what am I supposed to do?"

This is a throwaway scene. There's no reason for it to be there structurally. George doesn't learn anything from this conversation; Kramer doesn't either. It arrives between the urination crisis and the security office and doesn't advance anything.

And yet reading it, I keep stopping. It's as direct a statement as Larry David ever makes: yes, this is a show about nothing, but the "nothing" contains the question of whether dying bothers you and the practical observation that you're in a parking garage regardless. George's position — that being bothered by mortality is correct and appropriate and Kramer's equanimity is actively offensive — is stated plainly as philosophy, not as a joke setup. The laugh comes from the transition ("Meanwhile I'm here with you in a parking garage"), not from the anxiety itself.

The anxiety is real. Larry David just drops it in the middle, between two men looking for a car they can't find, and then moves on.

---

**The 360**

One stage direction near the beginning of the script: "FROM THERE P.O.V. THE CAMERA DOES A THREE SIXTY AND WE GET A SENSE OF HOW ENORMOUS THIS PLACE IS."

That's it. One camera move, written explicitly into the script as a deliberate choice, establishing scale as dramatic information. The problem isn't that they can't remember which level. The problem isn't that they were distracted. The problem is that this place is enormous, and the script wants you to feel that before anything else is established.

The 360 is architecture as argument. Then the camera comes back and they continue walking.

---

**What the episode is about**

Reading this, I keep coming back to the exchange between George and Kramer about mortality as the episode's hidden thesis statement. It's embedded so casually — between a bladder emergency and the introduction of a bodybuilder Spector with a fat fetish — that you can almost miss it.

But it names what the episode is *about*: the experience of time passing uselessly in a space that all looks the same, with things dying in your hands, in a hurry to get somewhere you're already too late to reach.

George's position is correct. Being in a parking garage and being bothered by mortality are not mutually exclusive. The question is what you do with both of those things simultaneously. Kramer shrugs. George can't.

The car is found. The fish are gone. The play is lost. The garage cost a fortune, and nobody said how much.

The silence before the fade-out is the answer.
