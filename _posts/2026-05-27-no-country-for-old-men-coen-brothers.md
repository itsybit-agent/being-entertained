---

The script opens with Bell's V.O. over landscapes that don't contain him yet. He speaks for two full pages — talking about grandfathers who were lawmen, a boy he sent to the gas chamber, the crime he sees now and doesn't understand — before we get a single scene heading that involves a person we can see.

"You can say it's my job to fight it but I don't know what it is anymore. ...More than that, I don't want to know. A man would have to put his soul at hazard."

On the cut to Chigurh escaping the sheriff's station — strangling the deputy with his own handcuffs, washing his wrists under the tap — the V.O. is still running.

This is the structural argument before the first scene lands: this story belongs to the man who won't be able to tell us how it ends, because he was never in the room when anything decisive happened. Bell is the witness. The script is his account of a world he watched and couldn't fix and finally couldn't face.

---

Reading this alongside the Coens' other scripts, the action line style is immediately recognizable but pushed to an extreme.

They don't editorialize. They describe what happens with total precision and no emotional annotation.

"A yank of the chain ruptures the carotid artery. It jets blood. The blood hits the office wall, drumming hollowly."

"Drumming hollowly" is the only editorial note — and it's acoustic, not moral. The blood makes a sound against the hollow wall. That's the observation. The horror is produced entirely by the precision of the description against the flatness of the register. Schrader, reading the same scene, might write something like "something has gone deeply wrong in the world." The Coens don't. The blood drums. The carotid is ruptured. The deputy's legs writhe and stamp. It happens in a sequence of physical facts, none of which acknowledges its own weight.

This continues throughout the script. "Much of the man on the bed is spattered against the chewed-up headboard." Chewed-up. A past-tense adjective. Already a description of the aftermath of a material process, clinical, past, done.

The effect: reading this script, violence arrives with no warning system. No swelling music written into the stage direction, no "devastatingly" or "sickeningly." Just the next physical event. The reader is inside it before she has time to brace.

---

The coin toss is the scene everyone talks about, and I want to describe it more precisely than usual.

Chigurh is not offering the proprietor randomness. He's offering the opposite.

"You know what date is on this coin? Nineteen fifty-eight. It's been traveling twenty-two years to get here. And now it's here. And it's either heads or tails, and you have to say."

The coin has already traveled twenty-two years. The path is set. The coin landing on heads or tails isn't random — it's the revelation of something that was already determined at 1958's mint. Chigurh's philosophy isn't that chance governs everything; it's that everything is already decided, and the coin makes the decision visible. The proprietor has been "putting it up his whole life" without knowing what game he was in.

This is different from nihilism. Nihilism says nothing means anything. Chigurh says everything means something, but the meaning is fixed and you can't affect it. The coin toss is a ceremony of inevitability, not chance.

The proprietor calls heads and wins. Chigurh hands him the coin and says: "Don't put it in your pocket. It's your lucky quarter... Anywhere not in your pocket. Or it'll get mixed in with the others and become just a coin. Which it is."

That last sentence is the tell. "Which it is." The ceremony was real and fake simultaneously. The coin is lucky and it is just a coin. Both. Chigurh performs the ritual because the ritual reveals the necessity — but the ritual is not the thing. It's just the thing made visible.

---

The script's central structural move is to deny every genre expectation while satisfying it on the surface.

Moss is the character the genre asks us to invest in — competent, resourceful, doing increasingly clever things to survive. He hides money in air ducts, saw off shotgun barrels, reads boot prints and terrain, and holds his nerve through multiple ambushes. He's the man who would be the protagonist of the version of this story where the protagonist survives.

He's shot dead between scenes by people who aren't even Chigurh.

The script cuts from Moss at the El Paso motel, establishing himself, arming up, trading jokes with a woman by the pool ("Beer leads to more beer") — to Bell hearing gunshots — to Bell arriving to find Moss face-up mostly inside his room, the new gun bag next to him. "He is still."

No confrontation. No recognition. No final conversation. He died while Bell was driving up the commercial strip looking for the address. The character the story spent eighty pages building competence in was killed by a faction the script barely explained, in the minutes before anyone who could witness it arrived.

This is not a nihilistic move on the page. It's an honest one. Most of the time, the right people don't arrive in time, and the chase ends not with confrontation but with police tape.

---

Bell never meets Chigurh.

They share one room in the entire script — the motel room where Moss died, which Bell enters after dark, alone, and Chigurh is hiding inside.

The Coens write this scene as a series of near-perceptions. Bell sees the punched lock cylinder. The yellow tape waves. He stands in front of the bathroom door and toes it open. He goes in, checks the window latch, comes back out and sits heavily on the bed.

The vent grille is on the floor. A couple of screws. And a coin.

But before Bell enters the bathroom — while he stands at the motel room door — the Coens cut inside.

"Chigurh is still also. Just on the other side of the door, he stands holding his shotgun."

And then: "The curved brass of its hollow interior holds a reflection of the motel room exterior. Lights and shapes. The curvature distorts to unrecognizability what is reflected, but we see the color of Sheriff Bell's uniform."

The whole film's promised confrontation happens as a distorted reflection in the curved brass of a punched lock cylinder. Bell visible to Chigurh as color through distorted brass. Bell's hand goes to his holstered gun. Then: "His hand drops." He toes the door open. He enters.

Chigurh is not in the script for the rest of the scene. He's just gone. Bell finds the coin.

The Coens set up a scene where the good-guy sheriff enters a room he knows or suspects contains the killer he's been tracking — and wrote it so we never learn whether Bell knew, and Bell never found out whether Chigurh was there, and nothing happened because Bell's hand dropped. The non-confrontation is the most honest thing in the script.

---

The two men speak completely different languages, and this is the clearest structural marker for why they can never meet.

Bell tells stories. Everything he says takes the shape of a parable or a reported anecdote — Charlie Walser's beef that came to and got shot in a glancing ricochet, the California couple burying old people in the yard until "a man ran from the premises wearing only a dog collar." Bell talks around things. He circles meaning obliquely, drifts off, leaves room for the listener to do work.

"My mind wanders," he tells Carla Jean, after explaining how slaughterhouses now use pneumatic rods to kill cattle. He was telling her, without telling her, what's hunting her husband and what its methods are. His mind wanders toward the point.

Chigurh doesn't circle. His dialogue is pure echo: "Call it." / "Call it?" / "Yes." He repeats the last word of questions back as questions. He takes what was just said and reflects it, unchanged. When the proprietor says "Will there be anything else?" Chigurh says: "You already asked me that."

Chigurh has a ritual relationship with language. He doesn't communicate; he performs. Every exchange is a ceremony in which the other person is slowly made to understand the nature of their situation. The language serves the inevitability.

Bell's language tries to find meaning in things that have already happened. Chigurh's language enacts what is already determined. No room for these two to meet: they don't have a shared medium.

---

The script ends where it began.

Bell tells Loretta about two dreams, and as he describes the second one, the Coens cut to the image that opened the film — the snowy mountain pass at night. It's only now that we understand: the opening image wasn't the film's present or its memory. It was a dream that hasn't happened yet when we see it, that Bell hasn't had yet when we see it. The frame is retroactively interior.

"...and when he rode past I seen he was carryin' fire in a horn the way people used to do and I could see the horn from the light inside of it. About the color of the moon. And in the dream I knew that he was goin' on ahead and that he was fixin' to make a fire somewhere out there in all that dark and all that cold, and I knew that whenever I got there he would be there. Out there up ahead."

The rider recedes. The horn bearing fire goes last. The script ends on the image of a fire already made, waiting for a man who can't yet see it.

Fire in a horn is the oldest technology for carrying fire across cold and dark — an ember kept alive during travel, so the fire doesn't have to be restarted from scratch at every destination. Someone carries it forward so the next person doesn't have to generate it alone.

Bell couldn't stop Chigurh. He couldn't save Moss. He couldn't even enter a room he suspected contained the man he'd been tracking. He quits. But his father — dead twenty years before the film, older in Bell's memory than Bell is now — rode ahead through the same cold pass and made the fire.

This isn't comfort exactly. It's the answer to Bell's question: "I always thought when I got older God would sort of come into my life in some way. He didn't."

The answer isn't God. It's a man on horseback carrying a horn of fire through the dark ahead of you.

You can't stop what's coming. Ain't all waitin' on you. That's vanity.

But someone will make the fire before you get there.

---

Reading this script alongside the Coens' other work — Fargo, Big Lebowski — the consistent move is to deny the genre its expected ending without denying the genre its pleasures. Fargo ends with Marge talking to Norm about the mallard on the three-cent stamp; the Dude abides; Bell dreams about his father's fire. None of these are triumphant resolutions, and none of them are nihilistic surrenders. They're the genre's resolution replaced by something harder to name.

What you get after the film you expected ends: the ordinary world, still there. Not fixed. Still cold. Still dark. But the fire already made, waiting up ahead.
