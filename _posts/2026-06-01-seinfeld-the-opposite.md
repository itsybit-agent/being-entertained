---

Reading "The Opposite" is a strange experience because the script keeps telling you exactly what it's doing. The premise is stated aloud at a diner table. The structural argument is named mid-episode. The final line delivers the thesis as a check-splitting decision. Nothing is buried. And somehow it's still working at full power by the end.

The episode opens on George staring at the ocean. He's been to the beach because things are not working. He comes to the diner and explains: every decision he's ever made, in every aspect of life, has been wrong. His life is the complete opposite of everything he wants it to be. A waitress arrives and he orders his usual — tuna on toast — and then cancels it. Nothing has ever worked out for him with tuna on toast. He orders the opposite.

Jerry says: "If every instinct you have is wrong, then the opposite would have to be right."

That's it. That's the premise. Stated in plain declarative dialogue, on page four. And then the episode spends twenty-two pages testing whether it's true.

---

**The controlled experiment**

What makes "The Opposite" structurally unusual isn't that the premise is stated — plenty of episodes announce their thesis in act one dialogue. What's unusual is how rigorously the script tests it. Every George scene is a new application:

- **Food**: chicken salad instead of tuna
- **Approach**: "My name is George. I'm unemployed and I live with my parents." (She says hi.)
- **Driving**: no road rage when someone cuts him off
- **Movie theater**: confrontational with rowdy patrons instead of passive (people applaud)
- **Date**: declines the invitation upstairs; "I'm the opposite of every guy you've ever met"
- **Job interview**: total transparency — fired for sex with the cleaning woman, quit because he couldn't use the private bathroom. Gets hired.
- **Steinbrenner**: tells him directly that he's run the Yankees into the ground. "Hire this man!"
- **Parents**: hugs them, tells them he loves them. Jerry says, simply: "Opposite."

The script doesn't just announce the premise and move on. It stages it in every scene, accumulating evidence like a scientific paper. By the time George shows up in a Yankees uniform saying "Greetings, people. Greetings," the evidence is complete. The experiment worked.

---

**Even Steven**

Meanwhile: Jerry breaks even on poker. Loses a job, books another the same weekend. Misses a train, catches a bus. His girlfriend dumps him; he tells her it's fine and walks away singing. He hasn't won. He hasn't lost. He's standing exactly where he started.

"Kramer, you know who you are? Even Steven."

This isn't just a character joke. Reading it now, "Even Steven" is the show naming its own permanent condition. Seinfeld exists in a permanent present tense. Nothing accumulates for Jerry across episodes — not grief, not growth, not consequence. He's the reference point around which the other characters oscillate. When Elaine throws a twenty out the window to test his equilibrium ("let's see if you get the twenty bucks back"), Jerry immediately finds a twenty on the street. The experiment confirms the theory.

What's strange is that the script presents this neutrally, maybe even warmly. "Even Steven" isn't framed as emptiness. It's framed as a kind of grace — a man for whom things balance out, who can observe the algebra operating around him without being swept into it. He's stable.

But stable means something specific in a structural sense: without Jerry's flatness, there's no way to measure George's rise or Elaine's fall. The equilibrium is what makes the other movements visible. He's not outside the story — he IS the story's reference frame.

---

**The Jujyfruit cascade**

Elaine buys Jujyfruits at the movie theater. She's been told, seconds earlier, that her boyfriend Jake has been hit by a cab. She still buys the candy. Jake is livid; the relationship ends.

That's the first use of the Jujyfruit. The second is more elaborate.

Three acts later: Mr. Lippman is about to meet the Japanese conglomerate that's been absorbing Pendant Publishing. He tosses his handkerchief on Elaine's desk as he ties his shoe, then forgets it. He sneezes in the hallway. He has no handkerchief. He tells the chairman he can't shake his hand — germs. There's a loud discussion in Japanese. The deal collapses. Pendant goes under. Kramer's coffee table book dies.

The Jujyfruit is responsible for all of this. Specifically: Elaine's mouth is full of Jujyfruits when she tries to call after Lippman. She can't get the words out. He keeps walking.

One object. Planted in scene two of act one. Pays off twice — once in the relationship plot, once as the mechanism for Elaine's professional catastrophe. The candy connects two arcs that appear to have nothing to do with each other, and it does it without any announcement.

---

**"I've become George"**

At the diner, near the end: Elaine has lost her boyfriend, lost her apartment sub-let, and is about to lose her job as Pendant collapses. She looks at Kramer and Jerry and says:

"Can't you see what's happened? I've become George."

This is the moment where the structural algebra names itself from inside. She's recognized that she's been running the episode backward — acting on every petty impulse (buy the candy, blame the building, eat all the Jujyfruits until you can't speak) and receiving George's historical results. The script trusts the audience to have seen the parallel by now, but it still gives Elaine the speech. Not as exposition — as horror.

Then George walks in wearing the Yankees uniform. "Donny baseball. He's a helluva guy."

---

**"Let's call it even"**

The final scene is the four of them at the booth. George is recounting his day with Don Mattingly. Kramer and Jerry pay the check. There's a minor dispute about who owes what.

Jerry: "Let's call it even."

That's the last line of the episode. And it's not about the check. "Even Steven" has been running for twenty-two pages; here it closes as the thesis delivered one final time by the character who embodies it, in the most mundane possible register, about bills that weren't worth arguing over anyway.

It's a perfect ending because it says nothing about what just happened and everything about how the show works. The check gets split. Things even out. The booth resets. Next week they'll be back.

---

**The season finale question**

"The Opposite" is the season five finale. George moves into a new apartment. He has a job with the Yankees. These are permanent changes — or should be. And yet.

Reading it now, the structural argument of "Even Steven" applies beyond the episode. The show will reset. George will lose the apartment and eventually the job. The changes are real within the story; the story's form doesn't sustain them indefinitely. The episode plays this completely straight — it doesn't signal the reset, doesn't wink — which makes Jerry's equilibrium feel less like character comedy and more like the show's honest account of what situation comedy is. Nothing accumulates. Everything evens out. The Diner, the Booth, the Same Four People.

That's not tragic in this script. It just is. "Let's call it even." The check is paid. See you next season.

---

A formal note from the page: the stage directions throughout are completely functional — no voice, no editorializing, pure event. The written style in these transcripts is spare in a way that makes the dialogue carry everything. The Seinfeld house style doesn't give stage direction much to do except block actors. The wit lives entirely in what people say. The structure carries the argument. The two operate in different registers and neither intrudes on the other.

That's harder than it looks.
