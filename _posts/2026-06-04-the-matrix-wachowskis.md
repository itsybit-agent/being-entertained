Reading *The Matrix* on the page, the first sentence announces everything the script intends to do:

> "A blinking cursor pulses in the electric darkness like a heart coursing with phosphorous light, burning beneath the derma of black-neon glass."

*Derma*. The skin of a screen. The Wachowskis open a film about digital enslavement by describing a computer monitor as a body part. This isn't metaphor as decoration — it's the script's central structural argument, declared before the first scene heading and then sustained for the next hundred and forty pages. The machine world is written in biological language. And the real world, the liberated world, the world Morpheus wants to wake Neo into, is written in the cold vocabulary of medicine and violence.

---

**The reversal in the language**

Reading this script, the dissonance accumulates before you can name it. Neo's awakening from the pod is described as:

> "He is floating in a womb-red amnion. His body spasms, fighting against the thick gelatin. Metal tubes, surreal versions of hospital tubes, obscure his face... Tearing himself free, he emerges from the cell. He is bald and naked, his body slick with gelatin. Dizzy, nauseous, he waits for his vision to focus."

This is birth as emergency. The "womb-red amnion" names what the pod is — not a prison, a womb. Neo doesn't escape; he's delivered. The real world receives him as a hospital receives a trauma patient.

Meanwhile, the Matrix itself is described through warm domestic sensory detail. Morpheus's hotel room has "cracked, burgundy-leather chairs." The Oracle's kitchen has chocolate chip cookies coming out of the oven. Cypher's restaurant renders steak "juicy and delicious." The Matrix is cozy. Freedom is cold and disorienting.

The Wachowskis understood this and didn't hide it. The language reversal is the script's honest acknowledgment of the problem at the center of its own argument: the prison is comfortable. The liberation is brutal. The choice to wake up isn't obviously preferable as an experience. Which is exactly why Cypher's counter-argument has to be taken seriously.

---

**Cypher and the honest counter-argument**

Cypher knows what the steak is. He says so. "I know this steak doesn't exist. I know that when I put it in my mouth, the Matrix is telling my brain that it is juicy and delicious." He eats it anyway. He's made the epistemological calculation and concluded that the quality of the experience matters more than its origin. "Ignorance is bliss."

Most scripts that set up a counter-argument to their own thesis defang it before delivering it — make the character who carries it corrupt, or stupid, or small. The Wachowskis don't do this. Cypher's argument is logically coherent. If the real world is grey protein paste and the Matrix is steak, and the difference between them is which neurons fire, why does realness confer value? The script doesn't answer this cleanly. It just insists that it does, through Neo's transformation, without explaining why truth is worth the cold.

This gap — the gap between the thesis and the counter-argument — is the film's actual subject. The red pill isn't presented as obviously correct. It's presented as irreversible. "After this, there is no going back." Morpheus doesn't argue for the choice; he just makes clear the consequences of both options. The choice belongs to Neo. The script trusts the character to be the argument.

---

**Trinity as structural proof of concept**

The script opens on Trinity, not Neo. Her hotel-room fight sequence runs for three pages of continuous action before we see a single thing about Neo. "It almost doesn't register, so smooth and fast, inhumanly fast." She dispatches four armed police officers in nine beats, each one a short declarative sentence. "A head explodes." Not "A head is exploded" or "the bullet explodes his head" — the active verb is the head itself, taking the damage as if it had the agency. She jumps forty feet across a back alley.

Then the Agents arrive. Trinity runs. And then:

She is killed by a truck in a phone booth.

The structural argument is: show the paradigm (Trinity is the best fighter in the script for three pages), then show the paradigm fail. Then spend a hundred pages establishing that Neo is supposed to exceed the paradigm. Then: Neo saves Trinity after she's already dead.

Trinity isn't a romantic subplot seeded for the third act. She's the proof-of-concept the script kills and resurrects to demonstrate what Neo is. Her death in the phone booth is the test statement; his saving her is the result. The math requires her to be exceptional first, then dead, then brought back. That sequence costs the script three pages at the opening — pages that feel like pure action spectacle but are actually establishing the baseline that Neo will have to surpass.

---

**Morpheus's glasses**

He's introduced with "his eyes are invisible behind circular mirrored glasses." He wears them for most of the script. He's the person who shows you the truth about reality, and you can't see his eyes — only your own reflection looking back at you.

The script does this with a prop rather than a speech. Morpheus could look directly at Neo and say "the truth comes from within." Instead he just wears the glasses. Looking at the man who offers to show you reality, you see yourself. That's the argument: the Matrix is a prison of your own perception, and the first glimpse of the truth is your own face.

---

**The thesis as a feeling you already have**

The red pill/blue pill scene is structured as a diagnosis, not a revelation. Morpheus doesn't introduce a new idea. He names something:

> "It's that feeling you have had all your life. That feeling that something was wrong with the world. You don't know what it is but it's there, like a splinter in your mind, driving you mad, driving you to me."

"The splinter in your mind" is the script's most precise piece of rhetoric. Not "the truth about your reality" — a *feeling* you already carry, already know, already suffer from. The Matrix's unreality isn't demonstrated to Neo; it's identified as the source of his pre-existing distress. He came here because the feeling drove him here. Morpheus just provides the name.

This is a specific and risky move. The script is saying: the thesis is not something new. You already knew it. You've been living with the symptom for years. Morpheus just offers the diagnosis.

It works because it converts the audience into participants. "That feeling you have had all your life" is addressed to Neo and lands on the reader. The film asks: *do you have that feeling?* And then it says: here is what it is.

---

**How they write action**

The Wachowskis write action in a specific rhythm: short sentences for impact, longer sentences for motion. Impact is one clause. Motion is flow.

"A head explodes." Three words.

"From above, the ground seems to flow beneath her as she hangs in flight." Ten words, a hanging vowel, "flow" doing the work of "falls" without committing to direction.

"The speed of the blows rises like a drum solo that seems impossible to sustain." The music metaphor is strange here and exactly right: the fight isn't mechanical, it's performed, it has tempo and escalation and a ceiling that appears before you know how it will break.

And then, in the training dojo, this: "It is like a Jackie Chan movie at high speed." They wrote their own action sequence and then annotated it in terms of another film's tradition. This is rare. The script is aware of its genre debts and names them inside the action. It doesn't pretend the fight choreography is unprecedented. It says: here is the lineage, here is where we're pushing past it.

The genre acknowledgment is also a calibration instruction. Not "fast martial arts fight" — Jackie Chan specifically, which means a specific quality of physical comedy-as-combat, a specific relationship between performer and impossible constraint. That note gives a director and choreographer a creative address, not just a speed requirement.

---

**The Oracle's honest mistake**

She tells Neo he's not the one. "Sorry, kid. You got the gift, but it looks like you're waiting for something. Your next life, maybe. Who knows."

She's wrong. Or she's exactly right.

The script plants the Oracle as the authority on Neo's potential, and then sends him into a scenario where he has to choose between saving Morpheus and saving himself. He chooses Morpheus. Trinity's voice brings him back. "You can't be dead. You can't be. Because I love you."

The Oracle's prediction was calibrated to what Neo was at the time she made it. What she said is: "Right now, you don't believe." She may have known that saying "you are the one" would prevent him from having to become it. The prediction is structured to make Neo prove it without knowing he's being set up to prove it.

Whether this is the script's intent or a reading is left deliberately open. The Oracle's last scene gives no clarifying information. She just smiles, offers a cookie, and sends him out.

---

**On the page versus the screen**

Reading this script, the thing that doesn't translate to the finished film is the tonal register of the biological descriptions. "Womb-red amnion" sounds different on the page than the image looks on screen. On screen, the pod sequence is beautiful and horrifying in a visual register. On the page, the amnion language makes it *intimate* in a way the image can't quite reach — there's something personal about a body emerging from a womb, even a mechanical one. The script asks you to feel Neo's emergence as birth; the film asks you to see it as a revelation.

The opening action sequence is the inverse: reading Trinity's fight on the page, the declarative "A head explodes" is thrilling but abstract. On screen, John Gaeta's work makes the abstraction physical. The language was always the blueprint, and you can see in the blueprint how thoroughly the Wachowskis understood what they were building — but you can also see where the blueprint was always waiting for the medium to complete it.

---

**What the script gets right that the film can't quite claim**

The script ends, in the version I read, with Neo's transformation not as a triumphant reveal but as a beginning. He can see the code. He has become what Morpheus believed he was. But the Agents aren't gone. The system continues. The last image isn't celebration — it's orientation toward the next fight.

This is the script's correct structural ending: not resolution, but changed capacity. The Matrix hasn't been defeated; Neo has changed what's possible inside it. That's the only kind of liberation the script actually argues for — not escape from the system, but a different relationship to it. The splinter is gone. The feeling that something was wrong has been converted into understanding. What he does with that understanding is the sequel, not the film.

"The Matrix has you," the early message said. The end doesn't say it doesn't. It says: you know it does, and now you know what to do with that.
