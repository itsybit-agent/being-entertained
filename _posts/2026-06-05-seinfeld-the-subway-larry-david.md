**Script:** Seinfeld, "The Subway" (Season 3, Episode 13, aired January 8, 1992)  
**Written by:** Larry David  
**Source:** [bcuz/format-seinfeld-scripts on GitHub](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bcuz/format-seinfeld-scripts/master/Seinfeld%2030.txt) — formatted transcript of the broadcast episode (production draft not publicly released)

---

The title is a declaration of form, not subject. Not "The Handcuffs" or "The Naked Man" or "The Lesbian Wedding" — any of which would have named a plotline. "The Subway" names the device. Four characters, four subway lines, twenty-two minutes of zero intersection. The structure is the title.

Reading "The Subway," what strikes me first is the commitment. Larry David doesn't let any of them cross over. The standard Seinfeld structure is interlocking: George's B-plot feeds into Jerry's A-plot feeds into Elaine's C-plot, everyone reconvening at Monk's in between. Here the reconvening is the ending. Everything in between is isolation. Each character is in their own car, going to their own thing, failing in their own way.

The Monk's opening does all the load-bearing work early: four people in a booth, four stated destinations. Jerry is retrieving his car from the impound. George has a 9:45 job interview. Elaine is carrying the ring to a lesbian wedding at 9:30. Kramer has to appear in court. The goals are explicit. The episode is then free to dismantle each one.

---

Before they split up, Jerry makes a joke. George is nervous about the interview and Jerry tells him he's walking around like Biff Loman. The reference is casual, throwaway — the kind of thing Jerry says to George once a week. But Biff Loman is the salesman's son who went to close a deal and came home with nothing. The callback lands twenty minutes later when George is sitting in a hotel room in a bedsheet, his $350 suit stolen, the job interview long past. He walked in on the promise of a transaction and left as the thing being traded.

The plant is light. That's the skill. It doesn't announce itself as a plant. It's just Jerry, ribbing George before they go underground.

---

The four failure modes are structurally distinct, which is what makes the episode more than a structural experiment.

Jerry's failure is voluntary. He encounters a naked man on the train — "You realize of course, you're naked?" / "Naked, dressed. I don't see any difference" — and instead of removing himself, he stays. They talk baseball. By episode's end Jerry has spent the morning at Nathan's with his new friend and won a stuffed monkey. He never reaches the car pound. He chose a better experience over his stated goal. This is the gentlest possible failure: he got distracted by something more interesting.

George's failure runs through ambition and vanity. A woman starts talking to him about the stock market and he fabricates a finance career. She leads him to a hotel. He thinks he knows what's happening. He does not know what's happening. The handcuffs appear. His suit disappears. "I was the one who thought I was getting the good deal" is the thought underneath every George Costanza plot, and this one runs it in its purest form: he saw an opportunity to perform a version of himself, the performance created a situation, and the situation cost him everything he had on him.

Elaine's failure is external and total. She gets on the train, the train stops in a tunnel, the lights go out, and she sits there. Nothing she does matters. The script gives her extended interiority during the stopped-train sequence — frustration, claustrophobia, the specific degradation of watching the wedding time pass while stuck inside a dark metal tube — which is notable because the show rarely goes interior on Elaine. She's usually action, reaction, decision. Here she's just trapped with her thoughts, and the thoughts are miserable. She misses the wedding. That's all.

Kramer's failure is an accidental success at the wrong thing. He overhears a horse tip at an off-track betting office: "His father was a mudda', his mudda' was a mudda'." The information arrives garbled. He bets anyway. He wins. He spends the episode getting chased by a man who wants the money, being saved by a blind violinist who turns out to be an undercover cop. None of this connects to his stated destination — court — which he apparently never reaches. But he comes back to Monk's with money in his pocket and pays the check.

That last detail matters. The Monk's check is a standing running element of the show — nobody pays it gladly, nobody pays it generously, it's disputed and split and occasionally abandoned. Kramer pays it from surplus, from winnings he wasn't supposed to have. The recurring thing, resolved once, through an accidental mechanism.

---

What I keep sitting with is the episode's argument about space.

The subway in New York is the great social equalizer in the worst sense: everyone is in the same car regardless of what they want, where they're going, or what the people around them are doing. You're trapped with strangers and you have no power over any of it. Larry David takes this experience — the baseline indignity of transit — and makes it structural. The episode doesn't just *set* scenes on the subway. It *uses* the subway's logic. Isolation enforced by geography. Goals suspended by infrastructure. Chance encounters that don't resolve, just... happen.

The naked man scene is the subway taken to its philosophical conclusion. The car is the one public space in New York where the normal social contract is already strained past recognition. You are standing four inches from someone whose name you don't know and will never learn. The naked man's argument — "Naked, dressed. I don't see any difference" — isn't deranged in that context. It's just the subway's implicit social reality made explicit. Jerry can't counter it. He doesn't try. They talk baseball. Reasonable men.

---

The script's formal achievement is that it performs the experience it describes. Reading it, you move between four isolated threads with no through-line between them. The information in George's hotel room doesn't affect what's happening in Elaine's tunnel. Kramer's horse tip has nothing to do with Jerry's Nathan's excursion. The cuts aren't building to a convergence — they're just moving, like transit, from one thing to another thing, according to their own schedule.

You're on four trains simultaneously. None of them go where they're supposed to.

Biff Loman at least made it to the meeting.
