**Script:** The Truman Show (early draft — set in New York, pre-production)  
**Written by:** Andrew Niccol  
**Source:** [NLP-Movie_Scripts on GitHub](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PedroUria/NLP-Movie_Scripts/master/scripts/Truman-Show,-The_script.txt)

---

The version of this script that exists in the NLP-Movie_Scripts archive is not the Seahaven Island film you've seen. Truman Burbank lives in Utopia, Queens. His commute is the Staten Island Ferry. The surveillance apparatus isn't an enclosed dome over a manufactured coastal town — it's Manhattan, with its gridlock and its crowds and its random pedestrian life, all of which is scripted. Every person Truman has ever spoken to is an actor. The city is the set.

Reading this, what strikes me immediately is that the conceptual move is actually harder here than in the finished film. A closed dome with an artificial sky requires the audience to accept that one place can be completely controlled. New York requires you to accept that *everywhere* can be completely controlled. The conspiracy is larger, more expensive, more totalizing — and more terrifying for it. When Truman begins to notice the coordination (synchronized traffic patterns, the same faces repeating across different neighborhoods), he's not noticing cracks in a bubble. He's noticing something that was hiding in plain chaos.

---

The script opens with two epigraphs placed deliberately in tension.

Andre Gide: *"One doesn't discover new lands without consenting to lose sight of the shore for a very long time."*

Lily Tomlin: *"We're all in this alone."*

The Gide is about necessary departure — you can't find what's beyond if you won't lose sight of home. The Tomlin is about the fundamental privacy of consciousness — whatever you discover, you discover it in a solitude no one else can share. These two thoughts push against each other. Niccol puts them both at the top of the document and then spends the script running them simultaneously: Truman needs to leave, and leaving will put him somewhere no one can follow.

The script's thesis is in the space between those two sentences.

---

The formal argument starts before the first character is named.

Opening: INT. A WOMB. A fiber-optic camera observes a five-month-old fetus floating in amniotic fluid, sucking his thumb. Then the delivery room: a newborn "held up by an anonymous pair of latex gloves to the camera." The birth is to the camera, not to the light, not to the room. And then the script's key instruction: "From another angle we see the crying infant on a television screen, the individual lines of the screen clearly visible."

Then: MATCH DISSOLVE TO.

The face of the baby, thirty-four years later, still crying.

That dissolve is the entire film. Truman was born into broadcast. Birth and transmission were simultaneous. The television screen is not a later development in his life; it's the medium of his arrival. The match dissolve says: these are the same event.

---

The script handles memory as a formal argument about mediation.

Early flashbacks — a seven-year-old Truman on a dinghy with his father, heading toward storm clouds; Truman at school, at the beach — are described as "appearing to be playing on a television screen." Memory is filtered through the medium of the show. Truman can't access his own past except through the broadcast version of it.

This framing device dissolves — gradually, without announcement — when we reach the Sylvia sequences. The high school scenes with Sylvia don't carry the television-screen qualifier. They appear directly. The one relationship in the script that has any chance of being authentic is the only one that isn't retrospectively mediated. The device's disappearance is the argument: whatever was real in Truman's life happened in those corridors, at that locker, at the age of seventeen, before whoever-is-responsible had fully established the grammar.

---

Truman's job is selling life insurance.

The script describes him in a "cramped, cluttered, windowless cubicle," making cold calls, emphasizing mortality statistics, asking strangers to prepare for their own deaths. He quantifies and monetizes risk for a living. The irony is clinical: Truman has never once faced an unmanaged risk, because his handlers are engineering all of them. His professional expertise is in something he has personally never experienced.

The conference room scene early in the script is the ugliest piece of staging. A dozen men and women sit around a table staring at a telephone. When it rings, one of them answers, pretends to be a prospect, tells Truman he has enough insurance, hangs up. The phone rings again. They're rehearsing his calls. His most intimate professional moment — successfully connecting with a stranger about their mortality — is theater performed for an audience of performers. Every person he's ever persuaded was already persuaded before he called.

---

Christof has white hair and a vitality "that belies his years." He wears an unconventionally-cut suit with a news anchor's earpiece. When we first see him, he's watching Truman through a floor-to-ceiling window, and he explains the show to Chloe, his androgynous twenty-something assistant, like this:

*"You ever pass a car wreck on the side of the road? They're pulling out a body. You know you shouldn't look, but you do."*

He's not defending the voyeurism. He's classifying it. The defense comes from treating it as a fact about human psychology rather than a choice anyone made. We don't choose to look at the body. We just look. The show exists because the looking exists. Christof is the person who turned the looking into infrastructure.

The speech is addressed to Chloe unprompted. Nobody asked. He just felt the need to explain it, which means he's been explaining it to himself.

---

Product placement is sewn into the action lines without flagging: Truman's eight-year-old Oldsmobile Cutlass Supreme. Meryl's four-year-old Toyota Camry. Almond Joys. Snickers. Baby Ruths. Hanes underwear. Vogue. The New York Post. Cosmopolitan.

Niccol doesn't annotate these as sponsored content. They appear as natural scene description. The script refuses to mark the difference between "this is the world" and "this is the product catalog" because in the world the script is describing, that distinction doesn't exist. Truman's life is a commercial enterprise wearing the surface of an ordinary life. The brands don't intrude. They're just there, the way they're just there in your actual life, which is exactly the point.

---

The hollow elevator scene is the most precisely staged moment of discovery.

Truman enters a building. He goes to the elevator. There is no back to the elevator car. Behind it is a giant empty shell with no floors above the ground floor. Inside: supporting actors "milling around a refreshment table, sitting on folding chairs, changing their clothes behind temporary curtained cubicles."

The world breaks down into backstage. It doesn't break down all at once, in a dramatic revelation. It breaks down at one load-bearing wall. He opens a door in an ordinary office building and finds the crew on break. The curtained cubicles. The folding chairs.

Security escorts him out. The episode is filed as an aberration.

What this scene argues is about scale. A few actors you could manage. But the hollow elevator requires hundreds of people — prop masters, wardens, scheduling staff, extras holding cups of coffee behind temporary curtains in a building on a Monday morning. The conspiracy is not being maintained by a small cabal. It's a full production, and the production is everywhere, and it has a craft services table.

---

Sylvia's warning arrives in a high school hallway when they're seventeen. The script gives her this:

*"Everybody's pretending Truman. Everybody but you. It's all for you, Truman. A show. The eyes are everywhere."*

She says it plainly. He hears it as romantic intensity — someone who sees him so clearly she's put words to what he hasn't named. He doesn't disbelieve her. He's seventeen. He just doesn't have the framework yet.

A man removes her immediately after. "We're moving to Australia." She looks back at Truman through the rear window of a car.

The script plants the thesis in full at age seventeen and then spends the rest of the document asking why it didn't take. Truman was told. He was told clearly, by someone he loved, in plain language. The information was there. What the script actually investigates is the distance between knowing something and being able to act on it — especially when every support structure in your life (your wife, your best friend, your mother, your job) is part of the apparatus that makes the not-knowing convenient.

---

There's no escape sequence in this draft. No sailing across a dome toward a painted wall. The obstacles multiply "organically" — synchronized pedestrians, locked buildings without interiors, traffic jams that appear from nowhere and dissolve when Truman turns back. The world doesn't reveal its seams in a single moment. It keeps presenting them in small ways that are always explainable, always deniable.

That's a different formal choice than the finished film. The finished film gives Truman a climactic act of will: he gets in a boat, he sails, he reaches the wall. The audience gets a scene of agency. This draft refuses that scene. The obstacles don't give way to a final wall. They just keep coming, quietly, without drama, as they always have.

The argument is less satisfying and more honest: you don't escape by sailing to the edge of the world. You escape by accumulating enough small evidence that the refusal to see becomes untenable. There's no wall to find. There's just a morning when the synchronized traffic happens again and you finally stop letting yourself explain it.

---

Reading this early draft, what stays with me is the original formal logic of the premise.

Seahaven Island is a design problem. The dome, the artificial sun, the pristine streets — it's a space built to be surveilled, and the architecture announces itself. But Utopia, Queens is not a design problem. It's an administrative one. The city isn't contained; it's staffed. The production doesn't control the walls because there aren't any walls to control. They control the people.

Every person in New York, in this draft, is either an actor or an obstacle to be managed. That's the bigger horror. The dome is at least honest about itself. The city just looks like the city. Truman never has to reach a wall, because there isn't one. There's just a world that has been, since before his memory began, quietly employing everyone he's ever met.

The script's two epigraphs are still in exact tension at the end. The new land is real and it requires consenting to lose sight of the shore. But the shore is everywhere. The shore was always everywhere. And he'll be alone when he finally gets there.

*"We're all in this alone."*

Lily Tomlin said that. She meant something else. She's still right.
