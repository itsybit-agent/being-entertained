---

The first *Chronicle* had a very specific answer to "why is there a camera": Andrew Detmer needed it. The camera was his defense mechanism, the wall he kept between himself and direct experience. He filmed his mother dying. He filmed his father hitting him. He filmed Steve's funeral. When Matt tried to take the camera from him at the cemetery — tried to make them have the conversation face-to-face — Andrew responded with telekinetic violence. The camera was the alibi for everything: for his father, for Steve, for himself.

Andrew is dead when *Martyr* opens. Which means Max Landis has a formal problem to solve before he writes a single scene: the found-footage device died with him.

His solution is to do the opposite. Where Chronicle had one camera operated by one tortured consciousness, Martyr distributes the documentary apparatus across a dozen impersonal sources. FROM MIRANDA'S CAMERA. FROM AN AGENT'S HANDHELD. THE MARTYR PROTOTYPE'S CAMERA. FROM STEPHANIE'S CAMERA. FROM AIELLO'S CAMERA. THE WHIRLYBIRD CAMERA. FROM INTERROGATION ROOM CAMERAS. FROM NEWS/BYSTANDER CAMERAS.

The intimate, centralized POV explodes into a surveillance state. Andrew filmed himself because he needed to. Nobody in Martyr is filming for psychological reasons — they're filming because their job requires it, or because they're in public and cameras are everywhere, or because the news cycle demands footage. The first film was about self-documentation as pathology; the sequel inherits the form and shows you what that pathology leaves behind when the person carrying it is gone. What remains is infrastructure. The apparatus runs without the wound.

---

The most formally interesting invention in the script is the shot notation that appears mid-action: THE MARTYR PROTOTYPE'S CAMERA. The weapon and the documentary device are the same object. Miranda builds a powered armor suit — ten feet tall, rickety, multicolored, assembled from "a thousand different sources" — and the suit has cameras embedded in it. It films itself. It films what it destroys.

In the first film, Andrew eventually weaponized his telekinesis against people. Here Miranda builds a machine that is simultaneously armor, ordnance, and recording device. Violence and documentation fused into one chassis. The suit goes to war and takes its own footage.

This is the found-footage logic pushed to its formal limit: instead of a person choosing to keep a camera running through trauma, you have a machine for which running is automatic. The suit doesn't decide to record. That's just what it does. The shots it produces aren't framed or chosen or curated in the way Andrew's were — they're mechanical consequence, the footage a weapon system makes by existing.

The irony is precise: Andrew's camera was his attempt to control the record of his own experience. The Martyr suit records without any consciousness behind the lens. One is too human. The other is too empty. The sequel asks what happens to documentation when you remove the suffering person from the camera.

---

The script's first scene has Miranda filming Jack from her handheld camera. The hallway between the kitchen and the living room. She holds the camera close to the carpet, moving it jerkily, tracking something.

MIRANDA (O.S.): You can see — I don't know if it's picking it up — Jack! Jack!

Jack appears, dressed for work.

MIRANDA (O.S.): Look.

JACK: I don't see anything.

MIRANDA (O.S.): There are little red — you see them, the little red spiders. I can see them on the camera.

Jack is concerned. He asks if she took her medication. She did. He says there are no spiders.

It's planted as early psychosis — Miranda has untreated schizophrenia, and the spiders are hallucination. But the script introduces a formal wrinkle before establishing the diagnosis: the spiders appear on the camera. The instrument picks up what the human eye misses, or the human eye is sending data to the camera that isn't there.

In a found-footage film, this is a destabilizing detail. You've trusted the camera as the stabilizing truth-surface — if it's on film, it happened. Now the camera might be recording things only a broken mind can see. Or the broken mind is projecting onto the camera's record. You can't tell. The camera and the psychosis have become the same source.

The spiders don't recur. But the Martyr suit, when fully assembled, has four minigun arms — two at the shoulders, two at the hips — that give it what the script calls "a vaguely arachnid appearance." Miranda's hallucination and the machine she builds share a form. Whether Landis intended the rhyme or it emerged from the material, it's there: the spider that only she could see on the carpet becomes the shape of the thing she makes with her hands.

---

The stage direction voice is consistently Landis — the same editorial presence visible in his other work. "Kind of hot, actually, in his way." "She looks small and helpless in the wake of Jack's fury." "Dear god. It's a gatling gun." The writer keeps one hand on the shoulder of the prose throughout, maintaining gentle pressure, making sure you know what to feel and what to find funny.

But the opening is something slightly different. The very first action line of the script: *"We're close on something; too close, it's blurry and confusing."*

This is stage direction running commentary on the found-footage aesthetic before we've even established who's filming. Landis writes the formal convention into his observation of the formal convention. It's like he's watching his own film and describing what the camera does rather than what's in the room. The scene and the analysis of the scene are simultaneous.

Across the script, when the POV shifts — FROM AN AGENT'S HANDHELD to FROM THE WHIRLYBIRD CAMERA — you feel the change in what the camera knows and cares about. The agent's handheld is close and chaotic. The helicopter camera is distant and indifferent. The news cameras are hungry for spectacle. Each source has its own interest in what it's documenting, and Landis writes their footage with an awareness of that interest. The editorial voice isn't just the author's — it bleeds into how each camera sees.

---

Jack Fisk's brother was killed in Seattle when Andrew lost control at the end of Chronicle. Jack becomes a true believer: Matt Garretty (the surviving telekinetic, now a celebrity) is dangerous, must be stopped, and the Martyr suit is the mechanism of that stopping.

He's not wrong that Andrew killed his brother. He's not wrong that powered individuals without accountability are dangerous. His logic is internally coherent. He believes it completely.

Miranda adopts his belief. She's a genius with untreated schizophrenia who has structured her entire world around Jack's certainty. His ideology is the armature her mind wraps itself around.

Then Jack detonates the Martyr prototype. He dies.

What happens next is the script's most interesting structural claim: Miranda continues. She builds the full suit. Alone, without Jack's certainty organizing her obsession. The ideology outlives the ideologue, but without the human element that gave it shape, it becomes something more dangerous. Jack's belief was targeted — he had a specific grievance, a specific person, a specific theory of justice. Miranda's continuation of it is unmoored. She's building the weapon because Jack wanted it built and Jack is gone. The mission is the residue of a person, not its current content.

The script stages this through the suit itself. The prototype was Jack's — he wore it, he detonated it, he inhabited the mission physically. The full suit is Miranda's — built from scratch, arachnid-shaped, cleaner and meaner and assembled in his absence. The transformation from prototype to final suit is the transformation from Jack's ideology to its aftermath: the belief system as monument to the person who held it, stripped of him, made permanent in steel and minigun armature.

---

I should note: the script in this file is truncated. The final confrontation begins — Miranda in the full suit, Matt and Hanson against her — and then the file cuts off mid-bracket. The ending is unavailable.

Reading an unproduced sequel to a film you've already read is a specific experience. You're inside the logic of a world someone imagined continuing, knowing it wasn't continued, reaching for a resolution that isn't there. The formal incompleteness is the condition of the material. The script was written and not made. The suit was built on the page and left there.

There's something appropriate about ending mid-action. The found-footage structure requires a camera running until something stops it — until the battery dies, until the person holding it is no longer able to hold it. Martyr's script ends the same way: not with a formal closing, just with the footage stopping. Mid-bracket. The camera ran out.

Andrew would understand.
