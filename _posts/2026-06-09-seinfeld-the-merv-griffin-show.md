**Written by:** Bruce Eric Kaplan  
**Source:** [bcuz/format-seinfeld-scripts on GitHub](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bcuz/format-seinfeld-scripts/master/Seinfeld%20162.txt) — formatted transcript of the broadcast episode (production draft not publicly released)

---

Reading this script, what hits first is a formal argument disguised as absurdism: once you install a television format, the format starts making decisions.

Kramer finds the Merv Griffin Show set in a dumpster. "(hits his hand on the rim) Jerry, this is the set from the old Merv Griffin Show! They must be throwing it out. This stuff belongs in the Smithsonian!" He hauls it into his apartment — "you just bring it in sideways and (pop) hook it" — and begins to run his life as an episode.

This isn't a one-joke premise that gets exhausted in thirty seconds. Every major plot development in the episode happens on the set or because of the set. Jerry's confession of drugging Celia happens there. Celia's confrontation of Jerry happens there. Jim Fowler's hawk destroys the set there. The Merv Griffin apparatus isn't a backdrop — it's the mechanism of the plot.

---

The first thing the format does is transform conversation into performance.

When Elaine arrives at Kramer's apartment to complain about her "sidler" problem, Kramer stops her: "Ju — Excuse me. Yeah we're, talking this way." He redirects her gaze toward an imaginary camera. He's managing the sightline before she's finished her sentence. The guest slot has opened; she's in it now; she didn't agree to this, but here she is.

More precisely: Kramer is using note cards. And the note cards are wrong. When George arrives, Kramer reads: "Now, uh you and uh, Jerry dated for a while. Tell us ah... what was that like?" George stares at him. "That was the wrong card," Kramer admits.

This gag is more precise than it looks. The note cards exist in advance of the guests — they're prepared for the format, not for these specific people. The interview questions don't belong to anyone in the room. The format precedes the content, and when the content (George) fails to match the question, the format acknowledges the mismatch and moves on. It doesn't stop the show. The show continues. "All right, let's change the subject."

---

The episode tracks what happens to the format when the content runs dry.

Kramer's "show" works when the guests have material — Jerry's toy-drugging, George's pigeon problems. But between guests, the format still has to fill time. So Newman and Kramer are left alone on the set:

> NEWMAN: Lately, though, I've been buying the generic brand of waxed beans. You know, I rip off the label... I can hardly tell the difference.  
> KRAMER: We've officially bottomed out, mm.

"We've officially bottomed out" is Kaplan writing the essay inside the episode. The Merv Griffin Show in the early 90s, before cancellation, had reached this point — celebrity guests giving way to whoever would fill the hour. The format doesn't end when the quality drops. It just accommodates the drop. The music still plays. The host still sits behind the desk.

Then: "We need a new format. Scandals and Animals."

---

This is the script's thesis arc, compressed into three scenes.

Merv Griffin (1962–1986/1991) ran on comfortable celebrity discourse. Johnny Carson's structure: the desk, the couch, the band, the chat. Entertainment that didn't demand anything of the guest or the viewer. Late in its run, the show scraped for material.

Jerry Springer (1991–2018, peaking mid-90s) ran on controlled confrontation: invite the person and the secret into the same room, surprise the person with the secret, let them collide on camera. Jerry says "I've done it a few more times since then" — admitting he's drugged Celia repeatedly — and then Kramer produces Celia from behind the curtain: "We have a little surprise for you! Come on out, Celia! It's the new format. Scandals and Animals."

The stage direction notes: "just like 'The Jerry Springer Show.'"

The Merv Griffin set has become a Jerry Springer set. The episode charts this as a transition, not a joke — the format evolved from celebrity chat to confrontation television, and Kramer replicated the evolutionary arc in twenty minutes inside his apartment on 91st Street.

Newman's role crystallizes here. He laughs at Jerry's confession. He cheers Celia's entrance: "Go girl!" Newman has been the studio audience the whole episode — the figure whose response tells you what register you're in. He's not a friend or an antagonist. He's the viewer who finds the worst behavior most entertaining. That's the Springer audience. Kramer's show has found its format and its people simultaneously.

---

The episode's most precise moment is six words in the stage direction: "Are we getting this?"

The hawk is attacking George and the squirrel. Slow motion. George's crossed arms. Feathers and chaos. And Kramer asks, off camera: "Are we getting this?" Not "Is George okay?" Not "Stop the bird." The production question comes first.

This is what the format has done to Kramer. He has been running a show for long enough that a colleague being attacked by a hawk reads as footage opportunity before it reads as crisis. The apparatus has reordered his priorities. He's not callous — he's formatted.

---

The ending completes the argument by showing the format escape the set.

The set is destroyed. Kramer dismantles his operation: "I'll tell ya it was a grind having to fill 10 hours a day. I'm not sure I was ready to have my own talk show set." The apparatus is gone.

Then: Elaine's apartment. Celia is there, drinking wine. Elaine pours her own full glass into Celia's glass — the exact gesture Jerry used to drug Celia, now performed by Elaine. The Merv Griffin theme plays.

The set no longer exists. The behavior it encoded has migrated. Kramer didn't have to be present for his new format to continue operating. The show's grammar — surprise the guest, control the chemistry, produce the revelation — has passed to someone else, in a different room, using wine and vulnerability instead of a stage and spotlights.

The format survived the set. Which is, on reflection, the more accurate claim about how television works. The set goes to a dumpster. The grammar keeps running.

---

Reading "The Merv Griffin Show," what stays is Bruce Eric Kaplan's formal precision about television's self-replicating logic.

The Seinfeld cast spent nine seasons being about nothing — conversations at Monks, the small transactions of daily life, petty grievances treated with the gravity of catastrophe. "The Merv Griffin Show" asks: what happens when a television grammar is installed inside that world? The answer is: the grammar colonizes everything it touches. Private conversations become segments. Friends become guests. Confessions become format requirements. The host's priorities restructure.

Kramer didn't choose to care more about the camera than about George. The show's structure made that choice for him.

"Are we getting this?" is the question the format always asks, whether or not anyone is filming.
