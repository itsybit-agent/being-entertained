**Script:** Magnolia (1999)  
**Written and Directed by:** Paul Thomas Anderson  
**Source:** [NLP-Movie_Scripts GitHub repo](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PedroUria/NLP-Movie_Scripts/master/scripts/Magnolia_script.txt) — script dated 11/10/98

---

Reading Magnolia, the structural argument announces itself in the first three pages and the last two pages repeat it — which means the three historical prologue stories aren't setup. They're the thesis, stated, then demonstrated for 180 pages, then restated.

The thesis is delivered in three iterations of a sentence that changes one word each time:

Story one (Green, Berry, Hill, 1911): *"And I Would Like To Think This Was Only A Matter Of Chance."*

Story two (Delmer Darion and Craig Hansen, 1983): *"And I Am Trying To Think This Was All Only A Matter Of Chance."*

Story three (Sydney Barringer, 1958): *"This Was Not Just A Matter Of Chance."*

The narrator's relationship to coincidence degrades across three stories. "Would Like To Think" — aspirational faith. "Am Trying To Think" — effortful skepticism. "Was Not Just" — surrender. By the third story, the narrator has given up the consolation that coincidence is what explains these things. Something else is in charge. The film spends its next three hours figuring out what.

---

The structural architecture is labeled explicitly. PTA uses title cards that read "Sequence A," "Sequence B," and so on through "Sequence K." These appear in the script as formal chapter markers — not hidden in action lines, not gestured toward through visual rhythm, but named. The reader sees the skeleton.

Between Sequences A and B, there is exactly one title card: *"Partly Cloudy, 75% chance of rain."*

That card is doing three things. It locates the day. It foreshadows the rain that runs for most of the second act. And it's a weather report, which is the genre of information that makes chaos seem predictable. A 75% chance of rain isn't certainty; it's probability. It belongs to the same intellectual family as "this was only a matter of chance" — the attempt to put numerical clothing on things that might not be subject to numerical description. The film opens on three stories about events no weather report could have predicted and then offers a weather report. The irony is not announced.

---

The camera is PTA's explicit narrator. Every action line that describes camera movement is a stage direction in the writing-as-filmmaking sense — he's not just blocking scenes, he's encoding editorial perspective. "CAMERA DOLLIES IN QUICK," "CAMERA WHIPS RT," "CAMERA moves INTO THE TELEVISION," "CAMERA moves in on his THROAT."

The camera enters Earl's throat and shows the cancer at work. The script describes this as a MEDICAL FILM look — the camera going inside the body the way a documentary camera goes inside a factory. He wants the same neutral register for cancer that a documentary gives to machinery. Then it pulls back out and Earl speaks.

When Sydney Barringer jumps from the building, the script draws NFL coverage diagrams in the action lines: "An x appears on the top of the building over Sydney... An ARROW is drawn that displays the PATH of his fall — a diagram is made to reflect the narration...like NFL coverage where the x's and o's and arrows and lines are drawn." This is in the shooting script as an element of the prologue, not a production note. PTA wants the analytical register — the post-game analysis, the instant replay — applied to a suicide attempt and an accidental shooting. The death is being diagrammed. The diagram is the joke and the tragedy simultaneously.

---

The "Wise Up" sequence is where the script stops pretending its characters are in separate stories.

It begins in Claudia's apartment. She's alone, looking at coke on her coffee table. Her stereo is playing a song. She hesitates. She snorts the coke. And: "she starts to sing along with the song."

Then the script cuts to every other principal character, each in their location, each starting to sing the same Aimee Mann song:

Jim Kurring, alone in his apartment, dressed up and ready to go. Jimmy Gator, alone in his office. Donnie Smith, alone in his apartment. Phil, in tears, at Earl's bedside. Earl, eyes closed, still alive. Linda, passed out in her car in a parking lot, head pressed against the glass. Frank, alone in his car, parked. Stanley, in a school library, light coming up from the book he reads.

The script's language for how this works: "The following has each of the principles half singing along with the song, who's lead vocal will stay constant throughout."

"Half singing along." Not full commitment. Not a number. They're surrendering to the song against their own intentions — Claudia just snorted cocaine, Jimmy is alone and confessing, Earl is dying, Linda is unconscious but her lips are moving. The singing isn't voluntary. It's what the pressure of the day has produced.

Stanley delivers the last lyric: *"till you wise up, no it's not going to stop, so just....give up."*

Then: *"The rain stops. Suddenly and quickly it's over. Clear as a bell."*

The transition is not commented on. The rain stopped because the characters admitted what the day has been about. This is PTA's claim about the relationship between emotional surrender and weather.

---

The frog rain arrives at the exact structural moment when every character's crisis has reached its peak simultaneously. Jimmy Gator is pointing a revolver at his own head in the kitchen. Linda has been rushed to the hospital by ambulance for an overdose. Earl is in his last minutes. Donnie Smith is climbing a ladder outside Solomon and Solomon Electronics to rob the place. Jim Kurring is driving with no gun on his hip, having lost his service revolver hours earlier.

The script writes the first frog landing as a single impact: "........CRACK........ From the sky, out of the blue, a large GREEN FROG lands on Jim Kurring's windshield."

The ellipses before CRACK are the only punctuation that arrives before the event. Not a warning. Not a build. Just time passing and then: frog. Kurring slams the brake. Looks up. More frogs.

Then: *"It starts to RAIN FROGS in the middle of Magnolia Blvd."*

Each character's frog rain is staged differently according to their situation. Rose's Jaguar slides and crashes. Claudia sees frogs fall past her window in the dark, a dozen in quick succession, knocks over a lamp, her apartment goes dark. Frank, at Earl's bedside, has his head buried in his dying father's hand and doesn't notice — Phil watches the frogs fall through the glass doors and says, in the script's flattest possible delivery: *"There are frogs falling from the sky."*

The ambulance transporting Linda's overdosing body swerves and falls over on its side. The frogs act as a lubricant on the wet street.

And in Jimmy's kitchen — Jimmy holding the gun to his head, about to pull the trigger, SOUND DROPS OUT — a frog falls through the skylight. Smashes through the glass. Lands on Jimmy's head. The gun goes off wildly. Hits the television. A spark catches in the wall socket and the house catches fire. Jimmy is alive. The fire will kill him later.

The frog rain saves Jimmy from himself and then kills him anyway, a few hours down the chain of events. The plague delays his death but doesn't prevent it. It just changes the mechanism from intentional to accidental. The narrator's thesis, demonstrated: this is not just a matter of chance. The frogs intercede in specific, consequential ways in every character's life. But intervention doesn't mean protection. It means participation in whatever is already underway.

The Donnie Smith sequence is the script's most structurally elegant use of the frogs. Donnie is climbing a ladder at the electronics store, about to attempt the robbery that the rest of the evening has been building toward. Kurring pulls into the same parking lot by coincidence — driving around in the aftermath of the frog rain that started twenty yards back down the street. Frogs start falling where Donnie is standing. He's knocked flat on his face. Kurring sees him, runs over, drags him under the loading dock shelter.

The frogs have delivered Donnie Smith into the care of the one police officer in Los Angeles who is likely to forgive him.

Later, after Donnie has returned the money and Kurring has covered for him, Kurring explains: "a frog falls from the sky and lands on the x-4 box 'round back and opens all the doors." The frog rain triggered the security system, which gave Donnie a way to return the stolen cash without breaking in. The universe did not protect Donnie from consequences. It created the specific mechanical conditions under which consequences could be avoided.

---

The Exodus reference enters through Donnie Smith, drunk in a bar, doing trivia theater for strangers. He's running through his old "What Do Kids Know?" material, quoting Shakespeare, when he says: *"The sins of the father laid upon the children is Merchant of Venice but borrowed from Exodus 20:5."*

This is not Exodus 8:2, the plague of frogs verse. But it is the Bible. And the sins-of-the-father line is the whole film's organizational principle — Earl abandoned Frank; Jimmy abused Claudia; Rick exploits Stanley; every parent-child rupture in the script runs on the same engine. The children carry the fathers' failures in their bodies and their behavior. Donnie — the quiz kid, the child product — quotes it from memory while drunk at the bar, as knowledge he was given at ten years old and has been carrying for thirty years without knowing what to do with it.

The Exodus connection in the frog rain isn't made explicit in this script. PTA embeds it through the film's accumulation of the number 82 in background details — license plates, scoreboards, clock times — which correspond to Exodus 8:2 (the plague of frogs verse). In the script itself, the connection runs through atmosphere and structural logic rather than annotated reference. The frogs fall. The inference is available. The script trusts the audience to make it.

---

Earl's last words to Frank are six words: *"You are not what you think you are."*

Frank thinks he is the man who built himself from nothing. The Seduce and Destroy tapes, the seminar, the persona called "Frank T.J. Mackey" — he has constructed an identity on the premise that his childhood was over when he walked out of Tarzana at seventeen. The journalist Gwenovier spends most of the interview trying to break through this, and Frank won't move: "Facing the past is an important element of not making progress," he says, quoting his own system back at her as a shield.

Earl sees through it. He has earned this sight through his own abandoned parenthood — he knows what it looks like to construct a life that doesn't include the reckoning you're owed. He is the origin of the thing Frank is running from. He looks at his son and what he sees is himself.

"You are not what you think you are." It could be an accusation. In the script's staging, it's a gift. Frank breaks down.

---

The film closes on Claudia. She's in bed in the dark, alone. Jim Kurring enters from behind and sits on the edge of the bed. She tears. Looks at him. Then:

*"She turns her eyes from him and looks INTO THE CAMERA and smiles."*

CUT TO BLACK.

This is the script's first and only direct camera address. After 180 pages of ensemble — cameras entering throats, diagrams drawn over suicides, every character's crisis staged in precise parallel intercutting — the film ends on a single person who has survived her worst night and looks directly at the audience and smiles.

She's not explaining anything. She's not performing. She's acknowledging that someone has been watching all of this, and that this is the face she has after everything the day produced.

The narrator opened the film in the position of someone arguing against coincidence, trying to decide what to make of it. The closing frame skips the argument entirely. It just shows what a person looks like on the other side of a hard night, when someone has entered the room and sat on the edge of the bed. The smile is the data.

---

Before the camera address, before the epilogue, the title card "So Now Then" arrives over black. The opening stories replay: Green, Berry, Hill hanging; Delmer Darion in the tree; Sydney Barringer on the rooftop.

The narrator returns: *"There are stories of coincidence and chance and intersections and strange things told and which is which and who only knows...and we generally say, 'Well if that was in a movie I wouldn't believe it.'"*

He's acknowledging the film you just watched. "If that was in a movie I wouldn't believe it" — addressed to a cinema audience watching the movie, about events that just happened in the movie. The narrator knows where he is. He's inside the film and naming it.

The thesis completes with: *"And it is in the humble opinion of this narrator that these strange things happen all the time."*

Not that they're significant. Not that they mean something specific. That they happen. Constantly. The three historical stories at the start aren't there to prepare us for an unusual film about an unusual day. They're there to suggest that this is the texture of ordinary life, which keeps producing frogs and coincidences and fathers who abandon children and children who come back to say goodbye, and nobody has a satisfying explanation for any of it, and that's what the weather report was never going to be able to account for.

The film ends. The book says: We may be through with the past, but the past is not through with us.

Claudia smiles.
