**Script:** "The Caddy" — Seinfeld, Season 7, Episode 12 (January 25, 1996)  
**Writers:** Andy Robin & Gregg Kavet  
**Source:** [Formatted transcript — bcuz/format-seinfeld-scripts on GitHub](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bcuz/format-seinfeld-scripts/master/Seinfeld%20122.txt)

---

Reading this script, the thing that keeps stopping me is the locked door.

George's car is in the Yankee Stadium parking lot for three days not because he's devoted but because he locked the keys inside and couldn't figure out what to do. The car just sits there, accumulating days, while George is absent. The Yankees organization misreads this. His dedication, his commitment, his going the extra mile — all of it concluded from the presence of a Buick that George couldn't retrieve.

He gets promoted.

The episode doesn't ask whether George deserves the promotion. It asks something more interesting: what does it mean when an accident produces exactly the same evidence as an intention? The car being there for three days looks identical, from a management standpoint, to a man who wakes up at six AM consumed by his work. The Yankees can't tell the difference. They read the signal — car in lot — and produce the consequence — promotion — and the consequence is real even though the premise was wrong. George didn't put the car there. The car put George there.

That's the structural engine of the whole episode. Not a lie, not even a misunderstanding — a coincidence that generates a misreading that generates real institutional consequence.

---

Running parallel to this: Elaine gives Sue Ellen Mischke a bra as a gift. This is explicitly passive-aggressive — Elaine thinks Sue Ellen looks ridiculous, and the gift is a correction delivered as generosity. Sue Ellen takes the gift and starts wearing it as a top, without a shirt over it. She walks down 83rd Street and Columbus. Kramer, driving, sees her. He crashes the car.

So: the bra goes from (1) passive-aggressive correction to (2) fashion statement to (3) cause of a car accident. That's three completely different categories. The object doesn't change. The context does, and each context produces a different meaning.

Jackie Chiles takes the case. His opening assessment is delivered in the exact register of Johnnie Cochran's civil rights vocabulary: "provocative," "lewd," "lascivious," "unfair." The language is borrowed wholesale from serious litigation and applied to a woman who wore a bra without a shirt. The form says: major case. The content is: a fashion choice we found distracting.

The courtroom resolution is the O.J. inversion. Cochran's most famous move was to bring the glove — to make the physical evidence demonstrate its own inapplicability. "If it doesn't fit, you must acquit." Here, Jackie brings the bra as evidence; the bra doesn't fit Sue Ellen in court. In the original, the garment not fitting was exoneration. Here, the garment not fitting is simply the destruction of the case. They have nothing. The physical evidence fails to demonstrate anything except that physical evidence can be wrong.

Cochran's glove won. Jackie's bra loses. The borrowed legal maneuver inverted.

---

Stan the Caddy is the episode's third structural engine, and he's the most precisely used.

He's introduced as a figure of quiet practical wisdom. His advice to Kramer early on — bring a lightweight jacket — is the kind of considered, experience-based counsel that the role promises. The caddy advises. The caddy knows the terrain. You trust the caddy's read.

And then Jackie pulls Stan into the lawsuit. Stan advises on the legal strategy. Bring the bra as evidence, he says. This is the catastrophically wrong call.

The irony is precise: a caddy's professional function is knowing what tool to use, matching the club to the situation, reading conditions. Stan does this correctly for golf. He does it catastrophically for law. He's in the wrong domain with the same posture of authority. The script doesn't explain this. It just has Jackie explode at the result — "Who the hell is Stan? You're caddy?!? This is a big mistake!" — and move on. The implication: expertise is not transferable. The man who knows when to use a 7-iron has no advantage in a courtroom.

---

The three Steinbrenner visits are the structure of the George plot made explicit.

First visit: George's dedication is recognized, praised, rewarded. The car is in the lot; the signal is read; the promotion is delivered. The misreading has consequences.

Second visit: George is on a road trip with Susan. He's gone. Steinbrenner, having believed George was always there, concludes from this absence that George must be dead. He calls the parents. He grieves.

Third visit: George reappears, alive. Steinbrenner, who was just grieving a loyal employee, is now confronted with someone who was alive all along — and docks his pay for the missed time.

The structure of the three visits tracks the same category error in three phases: presence misread as dedication, absence misread as death, presence misread as deception. Each misreading has the same shape. The institution reads the evidence it has. The evidence keeps being wrong. The consequences keep being real.

George ends the episode somewhat worse off than he started, which is the standard, and rides it out with the resignation of someone who has seen this before.

---

The episode closes in Elaine's office. Two women walk in wearing bra-tops — what the J. Peterman catalog is apparently now calling the "Gatsby Swing Top" or something similar. They're enthusiastic. They want to go to lunch. Elaine says no, waits for them to leave, and makes a gun-to-the-temple gesture.

She gave Sue Ellen that bra to embarrass her. She ends the episode having accidentally launched a fashion trend that her employer is now selling. The correction became the look. The insult became the product line.

The passive-aggression completed its arc in the wrong direction entirely.

---

What Robin and Kavet built in this episode is a machine for producing ironic consequence through misreading. Three separate tracks, three distinct misreadings, each running the same structural principle:

- An absence reads as dedication
- A correction reads as fashion
- A caddy reads as counsel

In each case, the reading generates real consequences — a promotion, a lawsuit, a destroyed case — even though the read is wrong. The script's argument: in institutions, organizations, and social networks, the signal travels independently of the intent behind it. The car in the parking lot means something, and what it means is determined not by George but by whoever reads it.

George didn't put the car there. He couldn't get it out. The meaning was applied to it from outside.

The gun-to-the-temple is Elaine acknowledging the same truth. The bra made its own argument. She just handed it over.
