**Script:** Twelve Monkeys (1995)  
**Written by:** David Peoples & Janet Peoples  
**Inspired by:** *La Jetée*, a film by Chris Marker (1962)  
**Source:** [NLP-Movie_Scripts GitHub repo](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PedroUria/NLP-Movie_Scripts/master/scripts/12-Monkeys_script.txt) — Production Draft, June 27, 1994

---

The first slug line for the future world is: `INT. PRISON DORMITORY/FUTURE - ETERNAL NIGHT`

Not just "NIGHT." *Eternal* night. The temporal marker — `/FUTURE` — does what slug lines are supposed to do: locates us in time and space. But "ETERNAL NIGHT" isn't a location. It's a psychological state encoded into format. Before a single line of dialogue, before Cole has said a word or done anything, the structure of the script has told us what it's like to live there. Not dark. Not underground. *Eternal.*

Two lines into the description of that world, the Peoples write the central formal argument of the whole film. Cole wakes from a dream of a boy at an airport into "AN ALMOST COLORLESS 'REALITY' OF BLURRED EDGES AND ECHOEY SOUNDS, MUCH MORE 'DREAMLIKE' THAN HIS DREAM."

The real world is described as more dreamlike than the dream.

This inversion isn't atmosphere. It's the thesis. A time-travel film about a man who cannot trust his own perception begins by encoding that unreliability into the stage direction itself, before the character can suspect it about himself. The reader is inside the epistemological problem before the plot has started.

---

The dream appears four times in the script, the same event each time: a nine-year-old boy in an airport concourse, watching a shooting. A man in a Hawaiian shirt, bleeding. A woman kneeling beside him in "a gesture of enormous tenderness, a gesture of farewell." The P.A. system droning flight information.

The first time we see it: fragments. The boy's eyes, wide. The wounded man. The woman's face obscured.

Each subsequent appearance adds something new. The woman's face gets glimpsed — "She looks a little like RAILLY." A voice is identified — "definitely JEFFREY'S VOICE." The terrified traveler in the background resolves into someone recognizable — "a lot like DR. FLETCHER."

But the gesture remains constant. The woman kneeling beside the dying man. The tenderness. The farewell. Every addition is a recognition event — Cole has now *met* these people, so his memory reads back into the images what wasn't legible before. The facts of the scene don't change. What changes is Cole's ability to decode them.

This is an argument about what memory actually is. The Peoples aren't staging a mystery that gets solved. They're staging the process by which a person learns to read an image they've carried for thirty years. The airport was always there. The event was always the same. The faces became legible as Cole encountered the people whose faces they were.

---

Reading this script, what I kept sitting with is the bootstrap problem, which the Peoples stage as a feeling before it arrives as a logical structure.

Cole's central line — delivered early, before any of his claims have been verified — is: *"It already happened! I can't save you."*

Most time-travel stories operate on the premise that the protagonist has been sent back to change something. The dramatic question is whether they can. The Peoples' script refuses this premise at the level of Cole's own understanding: he isn't here to change the past. The past happened. He's here to gather information. Every scene where he tries to be more than an observer — where he tries to intervene, to prevent, to save — produces new trouble without producing new results.

The bootstrapping runs deeper than that. The gun Cole carries to the airport was always going to be at the airport. He is not introducing a weapon into a historical event. He is delivering the weapon that the event required. The mechanism he's trying to prevent is the mechanism he's participating in. This is staged as dramatic irony rather than revelation — the audience understands what Cole doesn't, which is that the question "can I stop this?" has already been answered, and the answer is the film.

"It already happened" is both the character's burden and the script's structural commitment. Everything will happen. The watching of it is the story.

---

The Peoples were working from Chris Marker's *La Jetée*, a 1962 film made almost entirely of still photographs. Narrated in documentary voice, the film moves through images of a man haunted by a single scene from his childhood — a woman's face on an airport jetty, a man falling, a feeling he didn't understand at the time. The film's thesis is that this childhood image, charged with mystery, becomes the organizing principle of an adult consciousness. He keeps returning to it. Eventually he understands what he was watching.

*La Jetée* is made of still images except for one brief moment: the woman's face, asleep, waking up, blinking. In a film of photographs, the one moment of movement is the one moment of life.

The Peoples inherit that logic. The recurring dream sequence is the still photograph at the center of their screenplay. Everything else moves — time jumps, institutional corridors, escape sequences, hostage negotiations. The dream stays still. The boy at the airport, watching. The Hawaiian shirt. The gesture of tenderness.

Cole's adult consciousness orbits that frozen moment. The film is the process of giving it enough context to become legible.

---

There's a structural move in the script I keep coming back to: the José subplot as institutional proof.

José appears in Cole's future cell — a fellow prisoner. Later, in the present, Dr. Railly is giving an academic lecture on "the Cassandra Complex," using historical examples of ignored prophets. She shows a slide: a WWI photograph. In the corner of the photograph, recognizable to Railly, is José.

This is the moment the script chooses to shift Railly from professional skeptic toward genuine uncertainty. Not because Cole is persuasive. Not because his behavior is compelling evidence. Because she finds archival documentation.

The Peoples understand something important about institutional epistemology: testimony from an untrusted source doesn't move the professional, even when it's accurate. What moves the professional is evidence that arrives through a reliable channel — a photograph, an archive, a formally documented record. José doesn't testify to Railly. He appears in the historical record.

The script is making a quiet argument about what kind of evidence changes minds. Cole has been telling the truth the whole time. It doesn't help. What helps is a slide in an academic lecture that happens to contain a face she recognizes.

---

The institutional frame structures Cole's entire experience. He moves from underground future prison → present-day psychiatric ward → underground future prison → 1996 → 1990 → Maryland suburb → Philadelphia → airport. Every environment is either an institution or a space constructed in the shadow of one: the hospital's corridor logic persists even when Cole is outside it, because everyone he encounters relates to him through the diagnostic apparatus.

The script handles this by giving Cole his most unreliable-seeming behavior inside the institution that exists specifically to classify unreliable behavior. In the psychiatric hospital, he attacks multiple people without apparent provocation, tries to escape through impossibly small ventilation shafts, delivers speeches about viral apocalypse. The hospital does its job: it classifies him. The classification is institutionally correct and factually wrong.

Reading this, I kept thinking about what the structure does to the reader. We're positioned inside Cole's perception from the first page — we share his dream, we follow his attention, we know what he knows. When the hospital classifies him as delusional, we're already past the position of institutional reader. We've been inside his head. We have information the classification procedure doesn't have access to.

The Peoples are playing with the reader's epistemic position the same way the film's timeline plays with Cole's. We can't be neutral observers. The dream made us witnesses first.

---

The future world is described as colorless, blurred, echoey. More dreamlike than the dream.

The past — 1990, 1996, the airport — is described as vivid. Bright colors. Specific visual detail. Big glass windows letting in light.

This inversion works because it's true to the logic the film is building. Cole has lived in the future for decades. It's the world he knows, the world that's ordinary to him. The past is where everything is heightened — too loud, too fast, too full of sensory information, too alive. It has the quality that new experiences always have: the intensity of the unfamiliar.

But the Peoples write it as the dream feeling more real than reality. Which is also true to the mechanism of trauma: the past that haunts you is more vivid than the present you're standing in. The airport from thirty years ago arrives in full color. The underground prison you wake up in every morning is colorless and blurred.

Cole's condition isn't just time-travel. It's the condition of someone whose most real world is a memory he can't reach and a future he can't change, living in an eternal night he doesn't recognize as home.

The script encodes this in the first slug line. ETERNAL NIGHT. Then: the dream is more real than where he wakes up.

Both sentences are the film.
