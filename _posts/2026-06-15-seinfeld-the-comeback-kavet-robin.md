**Script:** "The Comeback" — Seinfeld, Season 8, Episode 13 (January 30, 1997)  
**Writers:** Gregg Kavet & Andy Robin  
**Source:** [Formatted transcript — bcuz/format-seinfeld-scripts on GitHub](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bcuz/format-seinfeld-scripts/master/Seinfeld%20147.txt)

---

Reading this script, the first thing that strikes me is where it begins and where it ends: George Costanza, alone in a car, furious, thinking of the perfect response too late.

Scene one: "The ocean called. Running outta shrimp. Outta shrimp! That's what I shoulda said! *Dammit!*"

Scene last: "My wife's in a coma. Yeah? Well, the life support machine called and..." He can't finish it. A thought occurs. He shouts "Wait! Yes! That's what I should've said!" He turns the car around and heads back to the airport.

The loop is the argument. The episode doesn't end with resolution — it ends with the engine running. There is always a better comeback. It is always one beat behind you.

---

Kavet and Robin build the episode's structural logic from three necessary conditions for deploying the perfect comeback: you need the *line*, the *audience*, and the *moment*. All three simultaneously. Getting any two is trivial. Getting all three is the problem the episode spends twenty-two minutes exploring.

George has the line on page one. He drives to the health club and delivers "jerk store" to Jerry. The group in Jerry's apartment immediately offers competing alternatives:

> "Your cranium called. It's got some space to rent." — Elaine  
> "Hey, Reilly. The zoo called. You're due back by six." — Jerry  
> "Just tell him you had sex with his wife. That'll kill him." — Kramer

George explodes: "This is why I hate writing with a large group. Everybody has their own little opinions, and it all gets homogenised, and you lose the whole edge of it. I'm going with jerk store!"

The meta-layer is visible here: Kavet and Robin are Gregg Kavet and Andy Robin, two writers working in a television writers' room, having written this scene together. George's defense of the pure singular vision against group compromise is a comedy writer's lament delivered in sitcom format. The speech about homogenization is the most self-aware moment in the script, wearing its awareness as a joke about what a writers' room does to the original impulse.

George defends "jerk store" against all competing lines. It's his line. He owns it.

He also can't use it. Reilly isn't at the Yankees anymore.

---

The Akron scene is the episode's structural payoff, and it's meticulous.

George flies to Ohio under false pretenses — a snow tire promotion at Yankee Stadium — to get himself into a boardroom with Reilly. He stages the conditions: big plate of shrimp, starts eating publicly, engineers the setup. Reilly takes the bait. "The ocean called. They're running outta shrimp."

George stands. He cleans his fingers with a napkin. He says, "Oh yeah, Reilly? Well, the jerk store called. They're running outta you."

And Reilly fires back, without hesitation: "What's the difference? You're their all-time best seller!"

The comeback has a better comeback. George has traveled to Akron, engineered the audience, staged the moment — and the line loses. Not because it's bad. Because Reilly is fast and the universe doesn't owe George anything.

Then George, in desperation, reaches for Kramer's suggestion from Act One — the exact line he explicitly rejected: "I had sex with your wife."

Silence. A colleague leans over: "His wife is in a coma."

The plot that Kramer and Elaine were running — the living will, the coma movie, the anxiety about vegetative states — has nothing to do with George's errand in Ohio. But they intersect at the worst possible moment through a detail George could not have known. The universe placed "wife's in a coma" in Akron at exactly the moment George needed a nuclear option. He pulls the trigger. The universe was already there.

The convergence isn't coincidence as a structural convenience. It's the show's thesis delivered through collision: the comeback is always at the wrong angle to the available information.

---

The living will plot works as a mirror of the George plot, running the same logic in different genre grammar.

Kramer watches a movie about a coma patient and writes a living will. He needs someone to pull the plug. He asks Elaine — "calculating, cold-hearted businesswoman" — and she accepts, flattered. They sit with the lawyer running through scenarios. The scene is twenty-three lines of increasingly marginal existence:

> One lung, blind, eating through a tube. "Naw, that's not my style."  
> You can eat. But machines do everything else. "I'd stick."

The decision grid is funny but the premise underneath it isn't: Kramer made a legal document because he's afraid of the situation he didn't see coming. Then he sees the rest of the movie. The coma lady wakes up. He immediately tries to annul the will.

The irony arrives through the tennis ball machine. Milos's racquet goes wide. The racquet hits the operator. The operator falls against the machine. The machine rotates toward Kramer. Tennis balls strike him in the head until he slumps unconscious. He wakes up in a hospital bed, still groggy — and Elaine walks in, sees both wall outlets occupied, yanks a plug without thinking. The plug looks like life support. Kramer screams.

Elaine doesn't pull the plug. But she would have. She was the executor. The script built every brick of this and then has the laugh arrive from a detail about a full outlet strip.

---

The Elaine plot runs a different version: the gap between the person you imagine and the person who actually exists.

Elaine has never seen Vincent. She knows him only through his video picks — "The Pain And The Yearning," 192 minutes, "an old woman experiences pain and yearning" — and through late-night phone calls. She builds a vocabulary for him: "strange and beautiful and sensitive." She wears the VCR play button he mailed her on a chain around her neck.

Vincent is fifteen years old. He has severe acne. When his mother pushes the door open, he shrinks back: "No, my acne!"

Elaine's reaction — "Ahh-cnee" — is the deflation happening in real time. The specificity of the projection (a sophisticated aesthete, a kindred spirit, a man) and the specificity of the reality (a teenager in a plaid shirt) are not reconcilable. She takes the vodka from the grocery bag and walks off swinging it.

The VCR button is the episode's tidiest image. A relationship so thoroughly imaginary that the most intimate object it produced is a component removed from a machine.

---

The Jerry plot is about dignity's operating radius.

Jerry agrees to let Milos beat him at tennis to protect Milos's secret — he can't actually play. The agreement has an implicit ceiling: Jerry will *lose*, not be *humiliated*. Milos keeps raising the stakes. "He's not a man, this Jerry." "Big baby." "He's not even married like I am."

Jerry's quiet warning: "I told you to cut it out."

He delivers one fierce serve. The chain reaction: Milos drops the racquet, the racquet arcs across the hall, the operator is knocked unconscious, the machine turns toward Kramer. Jerry's dignity costs Kramer a week of consciousness.

The script never calls this out. It just engineers the sequence. One true shot, four consequences.

---

The episode ends exactly where it began. The car. The fury. The better line that only just arrived.

The difference: the first time, the perfect line was "jerk store." The second time, it's the response to "his wife's in a coma" that George can almost see — he gets "the life support machine called and..." and then the words run out. A thought occurs. He turns the car around.

Kavet and Robin don't give him the line. They give him the structure of getting the line too late, again, and the behavior that structure produces: turn the car around, go back, try again. The comeback will always be behind him by exactly one moment. That's not a character flaw. That's just what it is to think of the right thing after the fact — which is what every human being does, every time, forever.

"Wait! Yes! That's what I should've said!"

The car turns.
