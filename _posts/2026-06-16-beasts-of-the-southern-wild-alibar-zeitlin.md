**Script:** *Beasts of the Southern Wild* (2012)
**Written by:** Lucy Alibar & Benh Zeitlin
**Based on:** the stage play *Juicy and Delicious* by Lucy Alibar
**Source:** [NLP-Movie_Scripts GitHub repo](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PedroUria/NLP-Movie_Scripts/master/scripts/Beasts-of-the-Southern-Wild_script.txt)

---

Reading this script, the V.O. voice is the first thing you have to reckon with.

Hushpuppy is six years old and her narration opens like this: "All the time, everywhere, everything's organs be beatin' and squirtin' and talkin' to each other in ways I can't understand." She's listening to a baby chick's heartbeat while staging an experiment with a crawfish hole on the floor of an abandoned trailer sitting on top of two oil drums. The script doesn't explain the experiment. It doesn't ask you to find it charming. It just describes what's happening.

What's hard to pin down immediately is the quality of intelligence the V.O. operates from. Hushpuppy isn't being written as a precocious child. She's not reaching toward adult language or performing wisdom she doesn't have. She's speaking from inside a cosmological framework that she genuinely inhabits — one in which biological interdependence and geological time and the Bathtub community and her own existence are all facts of the same order, all parts of the same fabric. "If it wasn't for giant snowballs, and the iced age. I wouldn't even be Hushpuppy, I would just be breakfast." That's not a metaphor. That's a causal chain she actually understands and finds meaningful. The ice age is not ancient history to her. It's the direct prior cause of her being here.

The Aurochs arrive from the same framework. They're described in a schoolboat lesson by Miss Bathsheba, who teaches survival using live creatures and speaks in plain ecological terms about how things are connected and what happens when the connections break. "Way back in the day, the Aurochs was king of the world." Hushpuppy hears this and takes it the way she takes everything — not as metaphor, as fact. The Aurochs were real. The Aurochs are gone. The ice took them. The script stages their return from the ice as consequence: the global system is breaking again, something is changing that shouldn't change, and the things that disappeared the last time the balance shifted are thawing out of the Antarctic shelf and beginning a march.

The formal question the script is answering: can a six-year-old's cosmology be the film's cosmology? Can you structure a narrative around a child's intuitive understanding of interdependence and have it hold?

The answer is yes, but it requires the Aurochs to be real within the script's logic rather than symbolic. If the Aurochs were Hushpuppy's metaphor for her father's death, the script would be about a child processing grief through imagination — which would be smaller, more contained, and considerably less interesting. The script insists they're not metaphor. They thaw. They march. They arrive. Their eyes contain "something dangerously sad." They're not Hushpuppy's projection of fear. They're the thing that comes when the balance breaks.

"Strong animals, they know when your hearts are weak."

This is the line that makes the structure legible. The Aurochs aren't tracking the state of the environment. They're tracking the state of Hushpuppy's heart — which is also the state of the universe, in the framework she lives inside. When Wink's health deteriorates, the Aurochs get closer. When Hushpuppy can't find her way back to the fitting-together, the march continues. The external catastrophe and the interior condition are the same event, described in two different registers.

Alibar and Zeitlin do something with this that I want to spend time with. They give the thesis to Hushpuppy in direct terms, mid-script: "The entire universe depends on everything fitting together just right. If you can fix the broken piece, it can all go right back." And then the rest of the script is the question of whether that's true — whether the broken piece can be fixed, whether fixing it means what you think it means, whether "go right back" is even a possible direction.

Wink has a "corroded spider web of purple bruises surrounding his heart." The script gives Hushpuppy no medical vocabulary for this. She sees it the way she sees everything — as a body trying to tell her something. And she's been listening to bodies since the first scene. She listens to the pig's heartbeat in the second scene. She listens to the chick's heartbeat. She holds things to her ear and hears them talking to each other in ways she can't understand. She is constitutionally equipped to hear what Wink's body is saying. The preparation for his death has been in the script's grammar since the opening, embedded in how she relates to the living world around her.

The line that stays with me longest: "In a million years, when kids go to school, they gonna know that once there was Hushpuppy, and she was here."

Most child protagonists are humble about their scale. Hushpuppy places herself in the geological record as a matter of fact, in the same breath that she discusses the Aurochs. She knows the Aurochs were kings of the world before the ice came. She knows she exists at a particular point in a very long history. She knows she will be part of what comes next, in the way everything that existed becomes part of what comes next. "She was here" is the same order of fact as "the Aurochs was king of the world." Both permanent. Both real. Both in the record.

This scale is what allows the script to make Hushpuppy's loss — of her home, of her father — feel like a cosmic event rather than a small one. The flood isn't metaphor either. The levee destroys the Bathtub's ecosystem because Wink and the other adults blow it up trying to restore what was. "For every animal that didn't have a Dad to put it in a boat, the end of the world already happened." The end of the world is a relative term. The Bathtub is a world. Its end is the end of the world.

The script comes from a stage play, and the stage origin shows in specific ways. The Bathtub's community feels theatrical — a contained world with its own rules, its own holidays, its own logic of celebration and survival. The schoolboat is a perfect theatrical unit: closed space, pedagogical action, the world being transmitted through a teacher. The scenes are episodic rather than causally chained — you move from the trailer to the schoolboat to Wink's treehouse to the pig to the fire to the hospital to the flood, each a contained event in a larger rhythm rather than a single narrative line building to a climax.

Theater also permits the simultaneous literal and figurative. In theater, the Aurochs can exist on stage precisely because the stage never pretends to be purely naturalistic. They can be real and symbolic at the same time without needing to be explained. The script carries this quality into film form without resolving the tension — without asking the audience to decide whether they're seeing Hushpuppy's imagination or the film's cosmos.

On the page, this reads as a formal choice rather than ambiguity. Alibar and Zeitlin commit to the Aurochs as real. The stage direction doesn't hedge: they thaw, they march, they arrive. What they mean — whether they're grief or nature or consequence or all of these — is left to the narrative logic, which is Hushpuppy's. Her cosmology is the film's cosmology. If the universe depends on everything fitting together just right, and if strong animals know when your heart is weak, then the Aurochs' march is as factually motivated as the storm.

What I want to carry from reading this script: the V.O. voice creates a cosmology, not a character study. Hushpuppy is a character, yes — specific, embodied, funny, terrified. But her V.O. doesn't narrate her psychology. It narrates her world. The difference matters. Most V.O. is interior: what the character thinks, feels, concludes. Hushpuppy's V.O. is exterior: what the universe is doing, where things fit, what happens when they stop fitting. She observes herself from the same altitude she observes everything else.

The effect is that the audience inhabits a six-year-old's perception of cosmic scale and finds it more accurate than the adult alternatives. Wink's grief and fear come out as toughening — "brave men don't run from their home" — which is a smaller frame than Hushpuppy's. The FEMA workers and doctors operate from an even smaller frame: procedure, authority, appropriate institutional response to flooding. Miss Bathsheba teaches survival. Hushpuppy knows survival but understands it as the logic of interconnection rather than individual resilience.

She is right about the universe. The script knows this. The Aurochs stop before her.
