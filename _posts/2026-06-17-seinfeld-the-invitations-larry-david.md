**Script:** "The Invitations" — Seinfeld, Season 7, Episode 24 (May 16, 1996)  
**Writer:** Larry David  
**Source:** [Formatted transcript — bcuz/format-seinfeld-scripts on GitHub](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bcuz/format-seinfeld-scripts/master/Seinfeld%20134.txt)

---

Reading this script, the thing that stays is not the death. It's how the death arrives.

Season 7 has been a long game about George trying to escape an engagement. He's written letters. He's floated Staten Island. He's introduced a pre-nuptial agreement hoping Susan will balk at the insult. She signs it immediately — she makes more money than he does, after all. Every scheme fails. The show keeps closing the exits.

Then George buys cheap envelopes. Not as a plan. As an instinct. He's in a stationery shop with Susan choosing wedding invitations and he steers her toward the discontinued stock with the inferior adhesive because the other envelopes cost more and George Costanza does not pay more if less is available. This is not George scheming. This is George being George in the background of a scene where bigger things are happening.

Susan licks the envelopes. The doctor calls it "toxic adhesive compound found in inexpensive envelopes." She's gone.

The thing the script understands: the exit George spent twenty-four episodes engineering couldn't arrive through engineering. It had to come from the thing he wasn't using as a weapon — the cheapness that predates the engagement, predates the show, predates any plan. The scheming was always going to fail because George's scheming is visible and compensated for. His cheapness is structural. It's not a tactic. It's him.

---

The death scene is written with clinical economy.

The doctor says "She's gone." George: "Wh—?" The doctor confirms she expired. George asks: "Are you talking about Susan?" The doctor says yes, and explains the toxic glue. George says: "We were expecting about two hundred people." Then: "Thank you. Thank you."

He doesn't weep. He doesn't collapse. He processes Susan's death in the register of the logistics that are now unnecessary — two hundred people, a venue, a date in September. He says *thank you* to the doctor. The emotional response the scene demands isn't available to him. He gives the response available to him: logistical deflection and courtesy.

In the hallway, the gang's reaction is brief. Elaine offers sympathy. Kramer says "Poor Lily." He's called her Lily all season. He still can't get it right at her death. This isn't cruelty — it's Kramer. Susan never fully registered in his reality as a named specific person. She was George's situation. Now George's situation has changed.

Jerry asks: "Dead?" Confirming the category. Then: "So I guess you're not getting married?" George: "Yes." The relief is barely concealed. It's not hidden. The script doesn't ask George to hide it.

---

Larry David makes a structural decision here that's the sharpest thing in the script: the characters don't give Susan the grief she deserves. They give her the grief they're capable of, which is almost none.

This has been read as callousness, and sometimes defended as a reveal of who these characters have always been. Reading the script, I think it's both, and also something more precise: it's the show making its thesis about its own characters explicit. Seinfeld has always been about people who are constitutionally incapable of appropriate emotional response. The premise isn't "people who are bad" but "people for whom the circuit between event and appropriate feeling is interrupted by self-interest." Susan's death is the event that most demands the circuit to work, and the script holds the interruption completely rather than giving the audience a moment of earned sincerity to break from.

This is Larry David writing the season finale of his own show, deciding what kind of show it is. Not an accident. Not a tonal error. A definitive statement. The characters don't transform. The comedy of their untransformability is the subject.

---

The B plot earns its place because it runs the same logic in a different register.

Jerry meets Jeannie at the crosswalk where she saves him from a cab. She criticizes his shirt collar — "sits for three weeks and it sticks up only half an inch." His first reaction is attraction, not irritation. Same initials (J.S.). She orders what he orders. She makes the same observations he would make. He thinks: "I've been waiting for me to come along."

He proposes. She accepts.

Then: "I hate myself. I hate everything about me." The relationship is a closed loop. Jeannie is a mirror and you can't live with a mirror. You need the friction of actual otherness — the person who reads differently, orders differently, sees differently. What felt like recognition was recognition: he recognized himself, which is not the same as finding someone to be with.

The episode places this subplot next to George's engagement ending and lets them mirror each other thematically without spelling it out. George spent the season trying to escape an engagement to someone who wasn't him. Jerry spent it — or this episode, anyway — entering an engagement to someone who was. Both are traps. The trap of being too far from yourself and the trap of being nowhere but yourself are structurally identical: you can't stay, and you don't know why.

Both relationships end in the same episode. Status quo restores. The show continues.

---

The pact matters.

There's a pact, never formally established, between Jerry and George: they're both outside the domestic institution. They both live in the same state of arrested bachelor refusal. The engagement threatened this. Jerry grieved it. And now:

"I'm engaged."  
George, weakly: "Hey. What can I tell ya."  
Jerry: "WE HAD A PACT!"

The pact didn't survive contact with the mirror problem. Jerry solved George's problem by acquiring his own. The solution to one man's loneliness gave the other man a compatible but identical loneliness. The pact is reinstated a few scenes later when Jeannie and Jerry recognize the impossibility and part ways. George calls Marisa Tomei. The call goes dead.

The show's ending isn't anyone escaping. It's everyone being exactly who they are, running another small loop back to where they started.

---

The thing I keep coming back to: the pre-nup failure is the episode's hidden hinge.

George brings up the pre-nup hoping Susan will be offended and call everything off. She laughs — "You don't have any money. I make more money than you do" — and agrees immediately. The exit closes. George sits in the apartment with a cigarette he can't smoke and a marriage he can't escape through any means available to him.

So the episode has to find the exit somewhere else. And it finds it in the envelope.

George didn't choose the toxic envelope as an escape. He chose it as a cost-saving measure. The thing that freed him is the thing he does reflexively, without thinking, in every context — he takes the cheaper option. He's been doing it the whole show. It's just that this time Susan is licking the cheaper option closed.

The scheming never worked because George's scheming is purposive and compensated for. His cheapness isn't a plan. You can't compensate for what someone is when they're not trying to be it.

The doctor says thank you for coming in.  
George says thank you.  
He takes the bus.  
The engagement is over.  
It was over because of an envelope.
