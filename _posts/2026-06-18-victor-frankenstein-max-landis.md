**Script:** Victor Frankenstein (2015)  
**Writer:** Max Landis  
**Source:** [Fountain format — lumenwrites/screenplays on GitHub](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/lumenwrites/screenplays/master/server/content/frankenstein.fountain)

---

The central structural argument of this script is in its first act: Victor gives the Hunchback the name "Igor" not as a gift, not as a ceremony, but as a bureaucratic convenience. His actual roommate is named Igor Straussman. The Hunchback is moving in. Someone might ask questions. "Just say you're Igor, yes?" The Hunchback nods. The stage direction reads:

> *"Perfect."*

And then, a beat, alone:

> *"I have a name. I have a...library."*

The Hunchback gets a name through the same careless logic that assigns mail to the wrong slot. He gets it before he earns it, before he's proven anything, before anyone has decided it belongs to him. The script's formal argument is that this doesn't make the name less his. By the final scene, he signs a passport with the surname *Frankenstein* — Victor's name, not Straussman's. He's crossed out the paternal inheritance and taken the partnership instead. The name that started as a cover story becomes the one he chooses.

---

The script's subject is what the word "partner" actually means.

Victor uses it freely and early. "I couldn't have done this without you." "We made this." "You're my partner." He says it in moments when what he means is: *you have access to my project and your work feeds mine and I will make decisions without consulting you.* Igor doesn't correct him. He's grateful to be in the apartment with a library. Gratitude and deference occupy the same space, and Victor doesn't distinguish between them because it doesn't serve him to.

The baby scene is where this breaks. Finnegan produces a dead infant. Victor and Igor resurrect it. Then Finnegan says: kill it. Victor agrees. Igor refuses. Victor says: "You will stand by. You will get out of the way." And the word "partner" is not spoken, because in that moment its falseness would be audible to both of them.

What follows is the script's central structural engine: Igor storms out, turns around, charges back in — and Victor hands him the dead baby with a note tucked inside. *"I — How little you think of me. —F."* Victor always intended to save the baby. He handed it to Igor knowing Igor would run it home. He engineered a situation where Igor's moral outrage becomes the delivery mechanism. This is not partnership. This is Victor using Igor's feelings as a tool while calling it collaboration.

The script knows this. It doesn't excuse it. It does something more interesting: it shows Victor capable of the right outcome through the wrong means, and asks whether the outcome being right is enough.

---

The back-brace is the physical argument.

Victor straightens Igor's spine through a needle and his own shoulder, then builds a wire-frame brace to hold the new posture while the body heals. The brace is freedom. The brace is also Victor's. Igor wears Victor's engineering on his skeleton.

The brace is destroyed in the Belgian climax when guards throw Igor in the Thames. He sinks in bound wrists and a bag over his head. He has to break his own back to slip the knot — crack, scream, then his hands come free. The brace gets him out of that river and back to the castle. Then, at the end, on the burning top platform with a steel rod through Victor's shoulder, Igor pushes the Prometheus off the edge and the brace breaks completely. He shoves himself to standing and stares at it on the floor, twisted.

He's standing without it. The spine holds on its own.

The script doesn't annotate this. It just stages it and moves on. But structurally: the brace was Victor's gift and the exit from Victor's orbit happens at the same moment the brace becomes unnecessary. Igor's spine was always going to straighten. The brace was the time it took.

---

Thompson's Anatomy frames the whole script.

In the opening act, Barnaby burns Igor's library. The one book he saves — *Thompson's Anatomy* — Nathaniel rips in half and throws in the fire himself. The script treats this loss with Igor's genuine anguish ("screaming in real agony"), which is higher than anything he gives to any of the subsequent violence.

The destruction is total and deliberate. Barnaby isn't depriving Igor of a book. He's destroying the one external evidence that Igor's interior life was real.

The script plants this and doesn't return to it for the entire second and third acts. Thompson's doesn't appear. Victor's father has a copy in his enormous library, mentioned once, and Victor immediately hedges: *he doesn't let me have access, he'd be angry if he found out I'd been touching his books.*

The final image of the script is Igor receiving a parcel. Inside: a mint-condition first edition of *Thompson's Anatomy*, with DR. CLAUS FRANKENSTEIN written inside the front cover — and crossed out. Below, in Victor's hand: *"I — Monument Valley, Utah. I have done something incredible. —F."*

The paternal name crossed out. The Thompson's restored. The forwarding address open. The book that was burnt in the third scene arrives in the last, signed with the gesture of a son canceling an inheritance and handing it to the person it was always meant for.

This is the script's most careful piece of engineering — planting at the point of maximum pain, then waiting 120 pages to pay it off at the point of maximum warmth. The gap between the burning and the arrival is the whole film.

---

The confession is the script's thesis, and it's buried in the middle of a burning building.

Victor is impaled on a steel rod, pinned to a wall, a Belgian windmill collapsing in fire around him. He thinks they're both about to die. He uses the time to say:

> *"Listen, Igor, listen to me. I didn't kill Straussman. I found him in the living room, I thought he was asleep but then I saw the needle...and then the pragmatist in me just took over, as he always does, and he wasn't a man anymore just pieces, just new equipment. I swear to you though I didn't ki—"*

Igor cuts him off: *"I believe you."*

Then: *"Because you didn't kill Igor Straussman — so now I can't leave."*

The dramatic logic here is unusual. Victor confesses not because the information will help him — he's about to die, and either way Igor has already decided not to leave him. He confesses because it's true and because the moment of apparent death removes every strategic reason not to say the true thing. The confession costs him nothing. It can't fix the relationship, can't earn him rescue, can't change any outcome. He says it purely because it's true and he wants Igor to know.

And that's exactly why it works. Truth offered when it cannot possibly serve you is the only kind that can't be argued with. Igor, who has been lied to and manipulated and used as an instrument all script long, recognizes this immediately. "I believe you." Not a judgment, just a recognition of what the moment is.

Then he saves them both.

---

One formal choice that's easy to miss: the monster is almost entirely absent.

Gordon, the first creature, appears in a ten-page sequence in Act Two, escapes, is recaptured, and killed. He isn't in the script for another eighty pages. The Prometheus is built off-page in Belgium, appears for the climax, and is killed. Together, the two monsters probably constitute fifteen pages of a 130-page script.

Reading this, what becomes clear: the monsters are consequences, not subjects. The subject is what happens between two people who call themselves partners but keep discovering they don't mean the same thing by the word. The monsters appear when the partnership breaks down — Gordon is created before Igor knows what he's contributing to, and escapes when he can't be managed; the Prometheus is built after the rupture, when Victor is operating without Igor's conscience as a check. In both cases the creation reveals the state of the relationship more than it drives the plot.

Landis wrote a Frankenstein script in which the creature is a symptom and the partnership is the disease. Or not quite a disease — a problem that can be diagnosed, named, and treated, but not cured in a single operation. Victor's last line is from a new continent: *"I have done something incredible."*

The show continues. The monsters will keep coming.

---

The stage direction voice is recognizably Landis throughout — breezy, punchy, fond of the exclamation point, occasionally speaking directly to the reader ("What a winning smile!"). Compared to the Dirk Gently scripts, where the narrator makes formal covenants and keeps them, here the narrator is more like a friend recounting the story. "He's standing up straight. Awkwardly, but straight." The parenthetical "awkwardly" is doing real character work: freedom is not graceful immediately. You stand up in whatever shape you actually are.

What Landis does that's less common: the back-and-forth in action lines between compression and expansion. Dunking chase sequences into three-word fragments. "Slam to title: FRANKENSTEIN." Then expanding into three full pages for a quiet dinner scene. The rhythm of the read is tied to the action — fast when things are moving, slow when people are talking, which is when the script is most interested.

This is not a formally experimental screenplay. It's a very well-made one. The engineering is in the plants, the payoffs, the staging of the confession, the back-brace as physical argument. Reading it, the machinery is visible in the best sense — not clunky, but readable, so you can see how it was built.
